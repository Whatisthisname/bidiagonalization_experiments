\section{Background}
While Bidiagonalization has well-understood forward algorithms and constraints, computing its gradient in a numerically stable way is challenging (TODO show that it is unstable from example). This paper builds from a connection between Bidiagonalization and Hessenberg factorization, namely that any Bidiagonalization can be computed by performing Hessenberg factorization on an appropriately constructed symmetric matrix, allowing us to then leverage the stable gradient of Hessenberg factorization proposed in (TODO cite Nico).

\subsection{Notation}
Let $e_j$ be the $j$th unit vector. Denote by ``$\circ$'' the element-wise 
matrix product which binds weaker than the standard matrix product and stronger than addition, and define the matrices
\begin{align*}
\mathbf{1} := [1]_{i,j=1}^K \qquad
I_{\leq} := [\delta_{i \geq j}]_{i,j=1}^K \qquad
I_{\geq} := [\delta_{i \leq j}]_{i,j=1}^K \qquad
I_{<} := [\delta_{i > j}]_{i,j=1}^K \qquad
I_{\ll} := [\delta_{i > j+1}]_{i,j=1}^K
\end{align*}
so that for example, $\mathbf{1} \circ A = A$, $I_{\leq} \circ A$ extracts the lower triangular matrix 
of $A$ (including the diagonal), and $I_{\ll} \circ A = 0$ enforces Hessenberg form.

\subsection{(Incomplete) Maybe overly complicated attempt at formalizing the sparsity notation }
It will be convenient to introduce notation to characterize the sparsity and dimensionality of vectors and matrices succinctly. We will use the symbol $*$ to denote an unknown scalar. Thus, $(*, 0, 0)^\top$ can be thought of as the span of the first unit vector. Formally, define an extension of the reals as $\mathbb{R}_* = \mathbb{R} \cup \{*\}$, where the sum and product with $*$ are extended as 
\begin{align*}
    s + * = * \quad\quad\quad
    s \cdot * = \bigg\{ \begin{array}{cr} 0 & s = 0 \\ * & \text{otherwise}  \end{array}
\end{align*}
Note that this implies $* - * = *$. Now, for $s, s' \in \mathbb{R}_*$, define partial order $$s \preceq s' \iff \square(s) \subseteq \square(s')$$ 
where
\begin{align*}
    % \square &: \mathbb{R}_* \to \{\mathbb{R}, \{r\} (r \in \mathbb{R})\}
    % \\
    \square(s) &= \bigg\{ \begin{array}{cr} \{s\} & s \in \mathbb{R} \\ \mathbb{R} & s = * \end{array}
\end{align*}
This implies $s \preceq *$. We extend $\mathbb{R}_*$ to vector spaces with an elementwise $+$, $\cdot$, and $\preceq$.

\subsection{Matrix free linear algebra}
Matrix-free linear algebra refers to implementing matrix operations without explicitly forming or storing the full matrices. Instead, we work with functions that compute matrix-vector products. This is particularly useful when dealing with large matrices where storing them would be impractical, or when the matrix is defined implicitly through some operation, where we never need the full matrix, only specific matrix-vector products.
For a linear operator $A: \mathbb{R}^m \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$, instead of storing an $n \times m$ matrix, we represent it as a function:

$$\text{matvec}: \mathbb{R}^m \times \Theta \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$$
such that $\text{matvec}(v, \theta) = A_\theta v$ for any vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^m$ and parameters $\theta \in \Theta$. This approach enables a general way to handle general linear operators, such as matrix-vector products with sparse and structured matrices, the jacobian-vector product of a neural network, finite-difference operators, discrete fourier transforms and other linear operators acting between vector spaces.

\subsection{Bidiagonalization}
$$\texttt{Bidiagonalize}_K: (A, v) \mapsto (L, R, B, r, c)$$
Bidiagonalizing an arbitrary matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times M}$ with initial vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^M$ and a prescribed number of iterations $K \in \mathbb{N}_+$, will yield column-orthonormal $L \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times K}$ and $R \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times K}$, bidiagonal $B \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}$, residual vector $r \in \mathbb{R}^M$ and inverse length $c \in \mathbb{R}$, satisfying the following constraints:
\begin{align}
    A^\top L &= RB^\top + r(e_K)^\top \label{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint1}
    \\
    AR &= LB \label{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint2}
    \\
    Re_1 &= cv \label{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint3}
    \\
    I_{\leq} \circ [L^\top L] &= I \label{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint4}
    \\
    I_{\leq} \circ [R^\top R] &= I \label{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint5}
    \\
    R^\top r &= 0 \label{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint6}
\end{align}
and $B$ is full rank and diagonally banded as follows:
\begin{align}
B = \underbrace{\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) + \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \text{k=1})}_{\text{B is 'upper bidiagonal'}} = \begin{bmatrix}
\alpha_1  & \beta_1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \alpha_2 & \beta_2 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
0 & 0 & \ddots & \ddots & 0 & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \alpha_{K-1}  & \beta_{K-1}\\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \alpha_K\\
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K} \label{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint7}
\end{align}
The full rank property on $B$ implies that no components of $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ or $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ can be zero.

\subsection{Hessenberg Factorization}
$$\texttt{Hessenberg}_K: (A, v) \mapsto (Q, H, r, c)$$
Given a square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ an initial vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^N$ and a prescribed number of iterations $K \in \mathbb{N}_+$, the Hessenberg factorization produces a column-orthogonal $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times K}$, 
structured $H \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}$, residual vector $r \in \mathbb{R}^N$, 
and reciprocal length $c \in \mathbb{R}$ such that
\begin{align}
AQ &= QH + r(e_K)^\top \label{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint1}
\\
Qe_1 &= cv \label{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint2}
\\
I_{\leq} \circ [Q^\top Q] &= I \label{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint3}
\\
I_{\ll} \circ H &= 0 \label{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint4}
\\
Q^\top r &= 0 \label{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint5}
\end{align}


\subsection{Hessenberg factorization generalizes Bidiagonalization}\label{sec:bidiag_through_hessenberg}
To compute $\texttt{Bidiagonalize}_K(A, v) = (L, R, B, r, c)$ for any $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times M}$ and $v \in \mathbb{R}^M$ using Hessenberg factorization, we construct the symmetric matrix $\dddot{A}$ and augmented vector $\dddot{v}$:
\begin{align}
    \dddot{A} := \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0} & A \\ A^\top & \mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{(N+M) \times (N+M)} \label{eq:A_sparsity_pattern}
\end{align}
    \begin{align}
\dddot{v} := \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0} \\ v \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{N+M} \label{eq:v_sparsity_pattern}
\end{align}
We define the operation $\texttt{augment}: (A, v) \mapsto (\dddot{A}, \dddot{v})$ as the augmentation of a matrix and vector to this symmetric matrix and padded vector.
Applying Hessenberg factorization for $2K$ iterations to this augmented input yields
$\texttt{Hessenberg}_{2K}(\dddot{A}, \dddot{v}) = (\dddot{Q}, \dddot{H}, \dddot{r}, c)$
which are directly related to $(L, R, B, r, c)$ through the following sparse equations:

\begin{align}
\dddot{Q} :&= \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0} & l_1 & \mathbf{0} & l_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{0} & l_k \\ r_1 & \mathbf{0} & r_2 & \mathbf{0} &  \cdots & r_k & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{(N+M) \times 2K}  \label{eq:Q_sparsity_pattern} 
\end{align}
\begin{align}
\dddot{r} &:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0} \\ r \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{N+M} \label{eq:r_sparsity_pattern}
\end{align}
\begin{align}
\dddot{H} := \begin{bmatrix}
0 & \alpha_1 & 0  & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
\alpha_1 & 0  & \beta_1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
0 & \beta_1  & 0 & \alpha_2 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
0 & 0 & \alpha_2 & 0  & \beta_2 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0 & \beta_2  & 0 & \ddots & 0 & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0   & \ddots & 0 & \beta_{K-1} & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \beta_{K-1}  & 0 & \alpha_K\\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0  & \alpha_K & 0\\
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2K \times 2K} \label{eq:H_sparsity_pattern}
\end{align}
The vectors $l_i$ and $r_i$ in $\dddot{Q}$ are exactly the columns of $L$ and $R$. $\dddot{H}$ is symmetric and banded, where the two bands hold the entries of the bidiagonal matrix $B$ with twice repeated interwoven $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ and $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ values. Recall that $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ and $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ were everywhere nonzero. The residual vector $\dddot{r}$ is a padded version of $r$, and the inverse norm $c$ is unchanged.
\\
We formalize this relationship through a linear extraction mapping 
$$\texttt{extract}: (\dddot{Q}, \dddot{H}, \dddot{r}, c) \mapsto (L, R, B, r, c)$$ 
that just projects away the zeros and extracts the relevant non-zero entries from the Hessenberg outputs.\footnote{The projection from $\dddot{H}$ to $B$ is not unique, as $\dddot{H}$ contains two copies of each $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$. We arbitrarily select the superdiagonal entries and drop the subdiagonal entries. This choice affects the sparsity pattern of $\nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho$ in section \ref{subsec:derivation_stable_adjoint_system}. Any other convex linear combination of each pair would also be valid.} This allows us to formalize \textbf{augmented Bidiagonalization} as $$\overline{\texttt{Bidiagonalize}}_K := \texttt{extract} \circ \texttt{Hessenberg}_{2K} \circ \texttt{augment} : (A, v) \mapsto (L, R, B, r, c)$$ which is functionally equivalent to standard Bidiagonalization but inherits the numerical stability properties of Hessenberg factorization's adjoint system. 
\\
The problem is that this is "computing a lot of zeros". We will show that we can prune the computational graph to minimize work.

\section{Adjoint systems}

\subsection{Adjoint system of Hessenberg Factorization}\label{subsec:adjoint_hessenberg}
Let $K \in \mathbb{N}_+$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times N}$, $v \in \mathbb{R}^N$, $H \in \mathbb{R}^{K\times K}$, $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times K}$, $r \in \mathbb{R}^N$, $c \in \mathbb{R}$ satisfy the primal constraints \eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint1} through \eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint5}. Let adjoint variables $\Lambda \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times K}$ with $\Lambda e_i = \lambda_i$, $\Gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{K\times K}$, $\lambda_0 \in \mathbb{R}^N$, $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}^K$ solve the following system of equations:
\begin{align}
0 &= \nabla_{Q} \rho + A^\top \Lambda - \Lambda H^\top + \lambda_0 (e_{1})^\top 
   + Q(I_{\leq} \circ \Gamma) + Q(I_{\leq} \circ \Gamma)^\top + r \gamma^\top \label{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint1}\\ 
0 &= \nabla_{H} \rho - Q^\top \Lambda + I_{\ll} \circ \Sigma \label{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint2}\\
0 &= \nabla_{r} \rho - \Lambda e_{K} + Q \gamma \label{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint3}\\
0 &= \nabla_{c} \rho - v^\top \lambda_0 \label{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint4}
\end{align}
The gradients $\nabla_A\rho$ and $\nabla_v\rho$ are then given by\footnote{note a sign difference between \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint6} and the result in (TODO CITE) due to a typo}:
\begin{align}
    \nabla_A\rho &= \Lambda Q^\top \label{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint5} \\
    \nabla_v\rho &= -c\lambda_0 \label{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint6}
\end{align}


\subsection{Solving the Adjoint System}\label{subsec:solving_adjoint_system}
The output gradients $\nabla_Q\rho$, $\nabla_H\rho$, $\nabla_r\rho$, $\nabla_c\rho$ will have know quantities, so we need only determine the adjoint variables $\Lambda$, $\Gamma$, $\lambda$, $\gamma$. $\Gamma$ is involved only in equation \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint1} in which only the lower triangular part is accessed, so for notational convenience we will redefine $\Gamma$ to be lower triangular, meaning we can effectively drop the mask $\Gamma := I_{\leq} \circ \Gamma$.
\\
We get expressions for $\lambda_K$ and $\gamma$:
\begin{align}
    \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint3} & \iff & \lambda_K &= \nabla_r \rho + Q \gamma \label{eq:get_last_lambda}
    \\
    & \;\overset{\eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint3}}{\implies} &Q^\top \lambda_K &= Q^\top \nabla_r \rho + Q^\top Q \gamma \nonumber
    \\ 
    &&&= Q^\top \nabla_r \rho + \gamma \nonumber
    \\ 
    & \iff & \gamma &= Q^\top \lambda_K - Q^\top \nabla_r \rho \label{eq:get_gamma_almost}
\end{align}
To evaluate $Q^\top \lambda_k$, inspect \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint2}:
\begin{align}
    \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint2} & \iff &Q^\top \Lambda &= \nabla_{H} \rho + I_{\ll} \circ \Sigma \nonumber
    \\
    & \;\implies & (\mathbf{1} - I_{\ll}) \circ Q^\top \Lambda &= (\mathbf{1} - I_{\ll}) \circ \nabla_{H} \rho + (\mathbf{1} - I_{\ll}) \circ I_{\ll} \circ \Sigma \nonumber
    \\ 
    &&&= (\mathbf{1} - I_{\ll}) \circ \nabla_{H} \rho \label{eq:get_lambda_in_Q_coords}
    \\
    & \;\implies & Q^\top \lambda_K &= \nabla_{H} \rho e_K \nonumber
    \\
    &\;\overset{\eqref{eq:get_gamma_almost}}{\implies} &  \gamma &= \nabla_{H} \rho e_K - Q^\top \nabla_r \rho \label{eq:get_gamma}
\end{align}
This final relation lets us express $\gamma$ in terms of the known quantities, which we can then plug into \eqref{eq:get_last_lambda} to get $\lambda_K$.
\\
Next, we will get expressions for the remaining columns of $\Lambda$ and $\Gamma$, as well as for $\lambda$. As a preliminary, from primal constraints \eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint2} we have $v = q_1/c$, yielding 
\begin{align}
    \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint4} \iff c\nabla_{c} \rho = q_1^\top \lambda\label{eq:get_q1_top_lambda}
\end{align}
We now turn to adjoint system constraint \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint1}, left-multiply $Q^\top$ and then apply a mask to consider only the upper triangular part of the equations:
\begin{align*}
    \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint1} &\overset{\eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint3}, \eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint5}}{\implies} \empty & \mathbf{0} &= Q^\top \nabla_{Q} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \Lambda - Q^\top \Lambda H^\top + Q^\top \lambda_0 (e_{1})^\top + \Gamma + \Gamma^\top
    \\
    \empty &\;\implies\; \empty & \mathbf{0} &= I_\geq \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{Q} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \Lambda - Q^\top \Lambda H^\top + Q^\top \lambda_0 (e_{1})^\top + \Gamma + \Gamma^\top\bigg]
    \\
    \empty & \empty & &= I_\geq \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{Q} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \Lambda - Q^\top \Lambda H^\top + Q^\top \lambda_0 (e_{1})^\top\bigg] + \text{diag}(\Gamma) + \Gamma^\top
\end{align*}
We now inspect column $i$ in this matrix equation. We will use sparsity of $H$ to rewrite the matrix product $Q^\top \Lambda H^\top$, and we will apply some simplifications with the mask $I_\geq$:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{0} &= \left(I_\geq \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{Q} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \Lambda - Q^\top \Lambda H^\top + Q^\top \lambda_0 (e_{1})^\top\bigg]\right)e_i + \left(\text{diag}(\Gamma) + \Gamma^\top\right) e_i \nonumber
    \\
    &= I_\geq e_i \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{q_i} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \lambda_i - Q^\top \Lambda H^\top e_i + Q^\top \lambda_0 (e_{1})^\top e_i\bigg] + \text{diag}(\Gamma)e_i + \Gamma^\top e_i \nonumber
    \\
    &= I_\geq e_i \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{q_i} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \lambda_i - Q^\top \left(\sum_{j=i-1}^{K} \lambda_j h_{ij}\right)\bigg] +(I_\geq e_i) \circ Q^\top \lambda_0 \delta_{i=1} + \Gamma_{ii}e_i + \Gamma^\top e_i \nonumber
    \\
    &= I_\geq e_i \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{q_i} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \lambda_i - \sum_{j=i-1}^{K} h_{ij}Q^\top\lambda_j\bigg] + e_1q_1^\top \lambda_0 \delta_{i=1} + \Gamma_{ii}e_i + \Gamma^\top e_i \nonumber
    \\
    &\!\overset{\eqref{eq:get_q1_top_lambda}}{=} I_\geq e_i \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{q_i} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \lambda_i\bigg] - I_\geq e_i \circ \left(\sum_{j=i-1}^{K} h_{ij}Q^\top\lambda_j\right) + e_1c\nabla_{c} \rho \delta_{i=1} + \Gamma_{ii}e_i + \Gamma^\top e_i \nonumber
    \\
    &= I_\geq e_i \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{q_i} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \lambda_i\bigg] - \left(\sum_{j=i-1}^{K} h_{ij} I_\geq e_i \circ Q^\top\lambda_j\right) + e_1c\nabla_{c} \rho \delta_{i=1} + \Gamma_{ii}e_i + \Gamma^\top e_i \label{eq:term_round_parentheses}
\end{align}
We can use \eqref{eq:get_lambda_in_Q_coords} to rewrite the term in round parentheses:
\begin{align}
    &\eqref{eq:get_lambda_in_Q_coords}:& (\mathbf{1} - I_{\ll}) \circ Q^\top \Lambda &= (\mathbf{1} - I_{\ll}) \circ \nabla_{H} \rho \nonumber &
    \\
    &\;\implies &  (\mathbf{1} - I_{<}) \circ Q^\top \Lambda &= (\mathbf{1} - I_{<}) \circ \nabla_{H} \rho \nonumber &
    \\
    &\iff &  I_{\geq} \circ Q^\top \Lambda &= I_{\geq} \circ \nabla_{H} \rho \nonumber &
    \\
    &\;\implies &  I_{\geq}e_i \circ Q^\top \lambda_i &= I_{\geq}e_i \circ \nabla_{H} \rho e_i \nonumber &
\end{align}
We plug this into \eqref{eq:term_round_parentheses}, which then yields an expression we can use to solve for a column $i$ of $\Gamma^\top$, requiring knowledge only of the value of $\lambda_i$:
\begin{align}
    \Gamma_{ii}e_i + \Gamma^\top e_i = - I_\geq e_i \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{q_i} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \lambda_i\bigg] + \left(\sum_{j=i-1}^{K} h_{ij} I_\geq e_i \circ \nabla_{H} \rho e_j\right) - e_1c\nabla_{c} \rho \delta_{i=1} \label{eq:get_Gamma_i}
\end{align}
\\
Finally, to solve for $\lambda_i$ and $\lambda$, we look again at \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint1} and restrict to column $i$:
\begin{align}
    \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint1} \implies & \empty & \!\!0 &\!= \!\nabla_{\!q_i} \rho + A^\top \!\lambda_i - \!\Lambda H^{\!\top}\!e_i + \lambda_0 \delta_{i=1} 
    + Q\Gamma e_i + Q\Gamma^\top e_i + r \gamma^\top e_i \nonumber
    \\
    &\empty &\empty &\!= \!\nabla_{q_i} \rho + A^\top \lambda_i - \!\sum_{j=i-1}^{K} h_{ij} \lambda_j + \lambda_0 \delta_{i=1} + Q(\Gamma + \Gamma^\top)e_i + r \gamma^\top e_i \nonumber
    \\
    \iff&  & \!\!\!\!\!\begin{array}{cc}
        - \lambda_0 & \!\!i = 1 \\
      h_{i, i\!-\!1} \lambda_{i\!-\!1} & \!\!i > 1
   \end{array}\!\!\bigg\} &\!= \!\nabla_{q_i} \rho + A^\top \lambda_i - \!\sum_{j=i}^{K} h_{ij} \lambda_j + Q(\Gamma + \Gamma^\top)e_i + r \gamma^\top e_i \label{eq:get_lambda_i}
\end{align}
We now have all equations necessary to solve for all adjoint variables. There is a part that we have not yet addressed: In \eqref{eq:get_lambda_i}, we will need access to $(\Gamma + \Gamma^\top)e_i$, however \eqref{eq:get_Gamma_i} will only give us $\Gamma^\top e_i$. How we do this is best explained with an example.

\subsection{Derivation of the Stable Adjoint System} \label{subsec:derivation_stable_adjoint_system}
As described in \ref{sec:bidiag_through_hessenberg}, we can use $\overline{\texttt{Bidiagonalize}}_K$ to bidiagonalize any matrix $A$ with initial vector $v$. The detour through $\texttt{Hessenberg}$ lets us use the adjoint system of Hessenberg Factorization described in \ref{subsec:adjoint_hessenberg}. In this section we will inspect the adjoint system of $\overline{\texttt{Bidiagonalize}}_K$, and simplify it considerably by exploiting sparsity in $\dddot{A}, \dddot{v}, \dddot{Q}, \dddot{H}$ and $\dddot{r}$.
\\ We will look at the constraints \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint1} through \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint4} and determine further sparsity patterns in the adjoint variables $\Lambda, \Gamma, \lambda, \gamma$. The following theorem summarizes the findings:
\begin{theorem}
    Let loss $\rho^\star(L, R, B, r, c) \in \mathbb{R}$ be a loss of the output of Bidiagonalization. Let $(A, v) := \texttt{augment}(A', v')$. Then, the adjoint variables $\Lambda \in \mathbb{R}^{(N+M) \times 2K}, \Gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{2K \times 2K}, \lambda_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{N+M}, \gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{2K} $ associated with a loss 
    $$\rho(A, v) := (\rho^\star \circ \texttt{extract} \circ \texttt{Hessenberg}_{2K})(A, v) \in \mathbb{R}$$ associated with are highly sparse. In addition to the constraints of the adjoint system given in \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint1} - \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint4}, for $i \in \{1, \dots, 2K\}$, they satisfy the sparsity relations
    $$\begin{array}{rrl}
        \text{For even $i$: }& \quad\quad \Lambda e_i \preceq& \big[\overbrace{0 \;\; 0 \;\; \cdots \;\; 0}^{N} \;\; \overbrace{* \;\; * \;\; \cdots \;\; *}^{M}\big] ^\top
        \\
        \text{For odd $i$: }& \quad\quad \Lambda e_i \preceq& \big[\overbrace{* \;\; * \;\; \cdots \;\; *}^{N} \; \;\overbrace{0 \;\; 0 \;\; \cdots \;\; 0}^{M}\big]^\top 
        \\
        \\
        \text{For even $i$: }& \quad\quad \Gamma^\top e_i \preceq& I_{\geq}e_i \circ \begin{bmatrix} * & 0 & * & 0 &  \cdots & 0 & * & 0\end{bmatrix} ^\top \vspace{10px}
        \\
        \text{For odd $i$: }&  \quad \quad \Gamma^\top e_i \preceq& I_{\geq}e_i \circ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & * & 0 & * & \cdots & * & 0 & *\end{bmatrix} ^\top
    \end{array}$$
    $$\lambda_0 \preceq \big[\overbrace{0 \;\; 0 \;\; \cdots \;\; 0}^{N} \;\; \overbrace{* \;\; * \;\; \cdots \;\; *}^{M}\big] ^\top \quad \quad \gamma \preceq \begin{bmatrix} * & 0 & * & 0 & \cdots & 0 & * & 0 \end{bmatrix}^\top$$
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Inspecting the sparsity patterns of $\gamma$ and $\lambda_{2K}$}
This section proves the statements $$\gamma \preceq \begin{bmatrix} * & 0 & * & 0 & \cdots & 0 & * & 0 \end{bmatrix}^\top$$ and $$\lambda_{2K} \preceq \big[\overbrace{0 \;\; 0 \;\; \cdots \;\; 0}^{N} \;\; \overbrace{* \;\; * \;\; \cdots \;\; *}^{M}\big] ^\top$$
Starting from \eqref{eq:get_gamma}, replacing $K$ with $2K$, we plug in our sparse gradients and sparse outputs:
\begin{align}
    \gamma &= \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_{2K} - \dddot{Q}^\top \nabla_{\dddot{r}} \rho = \nabla_{\alpha_{K}} \rho e_{2K-1} - \dddot{Q}^\top \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0} \\ \nabla_r\rho\end{pmatrix} \nonumber

    &\preceq

\end{align}
This shows that $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{2K}$ is alternatingly non-zero and zero, meaning it is at most $K$-dimensional. We plug this pattern into \eqref{eq:get_last_lambda}:
\begin{align}

\subsection{Sparsity patterns of $\Gamma^\top$ and $\Lambda$}
This section proves the remainder of the theorem, namely that
$$\begin{array}{rrll}
    \text{For even $i$: }& \quad\quad \Bigg(\Lambda e_i \preceq& \big[\overbrace{0 \;\; 0 \;\; \cdots \;\; 0}^{N} \;\; \overbrace{* \;\; * \;\; \cdots \;\; *}^{M}\big] ^\top\Bigg) & =: P_\Lambda(i)
    \\
    \text{For odd $i$: }& \quad\quad \Bigg(\Lambda e_i \preceq& \big[\overbrace{* \;\; * \;\; \cdots \;\; *}^{N} \; \;\overbrace{0 \;\; 0 \;\; \cdots \;\; 0}^{M}\big]^\top \Bigg) & =: P_\Lambda(i)
    \\
    \\
    \text{For even $i$: }& \quad\quad \Bigg(\Gamma^\top e_i \preceq& I_{\geq}e_i \circ \begin{bmatrix} * & 0 & * & 0 &  \cdots & 0 & * & 0\end{bmatrix} ^\top\Bigg) & =: P_\Gamma(i)\vspace{10px}
    \\
    \text{For odd $i$: }&  \quad \quad \Bigg(\Gamma^\top e_i \preceq& I_{\geq}e_i \circ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & * & 0 & * & \cdots & * & 0 & *\end{bmatrix} ^\top\Bigg) & =: P_\Gamma(i)
\end{array}$$
where we have abbreviated the propositions as $P_\Lambda(i)$ and $P_\Gamma(i)$. 
$P_\Lambda(2K)$ is already proven in \eqref{eq:lambda_2K_sparsity_pattern}.
To prove the rest, we will use strong induction from $i = 2K$ down to $i=1$, by showing that
$$P_\Lambda(i) \!\!\implies P_\Gamma(i)$$
$$\bigwedge_{j=i}^{2K} \big(P_\Gamma(j) \big) \!\!\implies P_\Lambda(i-1)$$

The remaining equations that solve for the unknown adjoint variables $\Gamma^\top$ and $\Lambda$ are the recurrence relations \eqref{eq:get_Gamma_i} and \eqref{eq:get_lambda_i}.
To show these, we will use strong induction backwards, starting from $i=2K$ down to $i=1$, by assuming that it holds for the current and all preceding iterations $j \in \{i, i+1, \dots, 2K\}$. The base case $i = 2K$ is already proven for even $i=2K$ in \eqref{eq:lambda_2K_sparsity_pattern}. Assuming now that the hypothesis is true for some even iteration $i$ (and all earlier iterations) we will show that it holds for the preceding $i-1$ which is odd. To close the loop, we prove that, when it holds for an odd $i$ and earlier, it will hold for the preceding $i-1$, which is even.

\subsection{Showing $P_\Lambda(i) \!\!\implies P_\Gamma(i)$}

\end{align*}

As can be seen, even columns of $\Gamma^\top$ are exactly zero in the odd entries. For odd $i$:

\subsection{Showing $\bigwedge_{j=i}^{2K} \big(P_\Gamma(j) \big) \!\!\implies P_\Lambda(i-1)$}
For this, we consider \eqref{eq:get_lambda_i} and plug in the sparse output of $\overline{\texttt{Bidiagonalize}}_{2K}$. We again underline the main terms with roman numerals (I) - (V), which we will investigate one by one, for even and odd $i$.

\subsection{The Adjoint Method to compute gradients}
The adjoint method provides an elegant way to compute parameter gradients of losses that depend on outputs of parameterized numerical solvers.
\\
\\
Let $s : \theta \mapsto x$ be a numerical solver that yields a solution $x$ to some problem $\theta$, with $\text{dim}(\theta) = P$ and $\text{dim}(x) = X$. Assume also that we know an associated constraint function $c : x \mapsto \mathbb{R}^X$ that evaluates all the constraints of the problem, for which $c(s(\theta), \theta) = \mathbf{0}$ for all $\theta$. Additionally, require that $\frac{\partial c}{\partial \theta}$ is everywhere nonsingular. Assume also that we have a loss function $\ell : x \mapsto \mathbb{R}$ that depends on the solution $x$ of the problem. We are interested in $\nabla_\theta \ell(s(\theta))$, the gradient of $\ell$ with respect to the parameters $\theta$.
\begin{example}[Hessenberg Factorization]
    A fitting example would be $s = $ \texttt{Hessenberg}, which implies that $\theta = (A, v)$ and $x = (Q, H, r, c)$. The associated constraint function would be the concatenation of constraints \eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint1} through \eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint5}. We see that, for this example, $c(x, \theta) = \mathbf{0}$ for all solutions $(Q, H, r, c)$ if and only if they correctly Hessenberg-factorize from $\theta$ = $(A, v)$.
\end{example}
Because $c(s(\theta), \theta) = \mathbf{0}$ is constantly zero, the total derivative $\frac{d}{d\theta} c$ is also zero. With the chain rule, we have:

\begin{align}
    \mathbf{0} = \frac{d}{d\theta} c(s(\theta), \theta) = \frac{\partial c}{\partial s} \frac{\partial s}{\partial \theta} + \frac{\partial c}{\partial \theta}
    \implies \frac{\partial c}{\partial s} \frac{\partial s}{\partial \theta} = -\frac{\partial c}{\partial \theta} \label{eq:exploit_constraint}
\end{align}
The adjoint method will exploit this relationship. We apply the chain rule to compute the gradient $\nabla_\theta \ell(s(\theta))$:
\begin{align}
    \nabla_\theta \ell(s(\theta)) = \frac{\partial s}{\partial \theta}^\top\nabla_x \ell(s(\theta))  \label{eq:gradient_chain_rule}
\end{align}
We assume that $\nabla_x \ell(s(\theta))$ is easy to obtain. The challenge is computing $\frac{\partial s}{\partial \theta} \in \mathbb{R}^{X \times P}$, which can be expensive for high-dimensional $x$. Suppose we can find \textit{adjoint variables} $\boldsymbol{\lambda} \in \mathbb{R}^X$ solving the uniquely determined $X \times X$ linear system (unique by initial assumption on the constraint Jacobian)
\begin{align}
    \nabla_x \ell(s(\theta)) = \frac{\partial c}{\partial s}^\top \boldsymbol{\lambda}  \label{eq:adjoint_system}
\end{align}
Then, substituting this into \eqref{eq:gradient_chain_rule} yields:
\begin{align*}
    \nabla_\theta \ell(s(\theta)) = \frac{\partial s}{\partial \theta}^\top \frac{\partial c}{\partial s}^\top \boldsymbol{\lambda} = \left(\frac{\partial c}{\partial s} \frac{\partial s}{\partial \theta}\right)^\top \boldsymbol{\lambda} \overset{\eqref{eq:exploit_constraint}}{=} -\frac{\partial c}{\partial \theta}^\top \boldsymbol{\lambda}
\end{align*}
The adjoint method provides significant computational savings compared to the standard approach. To see this, consider the two approaches:

\textit{Forward approach:} Compute $\frac{\partial s}{\partial \theta} \in \mathbb{R}^{X \times P}$ directly by solving $P$ linear systems of size $X \times X$ (one for each parameter), then compute the gradient via \eqref{eq:gradient_chain_rule}. Total cost: $O(PX^3)$ operations.

\textit{Adjoint approach:} Solve one linear system \eqref{eq:adjoint_system} of size $X \times X$ to find $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$, then evaluate $-\frac{\partial c}{\partial \theta}^\top \boldsymbol{\lambda}$. Total cost: $O(X^3 + XP)$ operations.

When $P \ll X$ (few parameters, high-dimensional state), the adjoint method is dramatically more efficient: $O(X^3)$ versus $O(PX^3)$. This advantage becomes crucial in applications like neural network training or PDE-constrained optimization where $X$ can be very large.

\subsection{Reprojection and Reorthonormalization}
Let $m$ be a mask vector with entries from $\{0, 1\}$. Let $L$ be an orthonormal matrix, and $q$ and $c$ be vectors. Consider a constraint of the form
$$m \circ (L^\top q) = m \circ c$$
Paraphrased: "Within the bounds of the mask $m$, we have that $q$ expressed in the basis of $L$ equals $c$".
If we have access to a numerical estimate $q_0$ of a solution $q$, we can get an improved estimate $q_1$ by \textit{reprojecting} it against $L$.
\begin{align*}
    q_0 &= q_0 - L (m \circ L^\top q_0) + L (m \circ L^\top q_0)
    \\
    &= q_0 - L (m \circ L^\top q_0) + L (m \circ c)
\end{align*}
The following motivates following update step, which can be repeated if necessary.
\begin{align*}
    q_1 &\leftarrow q_0 - L (m \circ L^\top q_0) + L (m \circ c)
\end{align*}
In the case of Hessenberg factorization, we have the constraint
$Q^\top q_i = e_i$ \eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint3} in which case we call the reprojection step followed by normalization \textit{reorthonormalization}.
\\
For Bidiagonalization, we have similar constraints $L^\top l_i = e_i$ \eqref{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint4} and $R^\top r_i = e_i$ \eqref{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint5}. Empirically, we find that two repetitions of the reprojection step are necessary to eliminate numerical errors. (TODO show this on a Hilbert matrix?)
