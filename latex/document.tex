\title{Numerically stable gradients of Golub-Kahan Bidiagonalization}
\author{Theo Würtzen and Nicholas Krämer}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Bidiagonalization is used throughout matrix-free numerical linear algebra, but differentiating through it with automatic differentiation is both inaccurate and memory-hungry: the backward pass drifts and exhausts memory long before the forward pass runs into trouble. We derive a numerically stable, matrix-free adjoint for Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization. The starting point is that bidiagonalizing a matrix $A$ with initial vector $v$ is the same computation as a Hessenberg factorization of the augmented symmetric matrix $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0 & A\\ A^\top & 0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, so we can borrow the stable Hessenberg adjoint of \citet{kraemer2024gradients}. That augmented problem is mostly zeros; pruning them collapses the adjoint back to the original dimensions and leaves a small system built from matrix-vector products and a reprojection step. In our experiments the adjoint costs about as much as the forward pass to compile and run, and it stays accurate on problems large enough that plain automatic differentiation has already given up.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Many models in scientific and probabilistic machine learning are tuned by differentiating functions of large matrices, where the matrix is available only through matrix-vector products~\citep{kraemer2024gradients}. Krylov methods such as bidiagonalization and the closely related Lanczos and Arnoldi iterations are the standard tools for these problems, yet differentiating the iterations themselves in a stable, matrix-free way has remained difficult.

Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization~\citep{golub1965calculating} suffers from loss of orthogonality of the computed vectors in $L$ and $R$~\citep{paige1971computation}, which is repaired in the forward pass by reorthonormalizing each new vector against the previously computed ones~\citep{golub2013matrix}. Differentiating the iteration is harder: reverse-mode automatic differentiation through the unrolled loop both loses accuracy as the matrix grows and stores the entire computational graph, exhausting memory.

This paper makes the gradient of bidiagonalization stable and matrix-free. We use that bidiagonalizing $A$ with initial vector $v$ is exactly a Hessenberg factorization of an augmented symmetric matrix built from $A$, so we can reuse the stable Hessenberg adjoint of \citet{kraemer2024gradients}. Most of that augmented problem is structurally zero, and pruning the zeros collapses the adjoint back to the original dimensions. What remains is a small system whose reprojection step does for the backward pass what reorthonormalization does for the forward pass. Section~\ref{sec:bidiag_through_hessenberg} sets up the connection to Hessenberg factorization, Section~\ref{subsec:derivation_stable_adjoint_system} carries out the reduction, and Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports the experiments.

\section{Background}
While Bidiagonalization has well-understood forward algorithms and constraints, computing its gradient in a numerically stable way is challenging, and naive automatic differentiation drifts as the matrix grows (Figure~\ref{fig:stability}). This paper builds from a connection between Bidiagonalization and Hessenberg factorization, namely that any Bidiagonalization can be computed by performing Hessenberg factorization on an appropriately constructed symmetric matrix, allowing us to then leverage the stable gradient of Hessenberg factorization proposed by \citet{kraemer2024gradients}.

\subsection{Notation}
Let $e_j$ be the $j$th unit vector. Denote by ``$\circ$'' the element-wise 
matrix product which binds weaker than the standard matrix product and stronger than addition, and define the matrices
\begin{align*}
\mathbf{1} := [1]_{i,j=1}^K \qquad
I_{\leq} := [\delta_{i \geq j}]_{i,j=1}^K \qquad
I_{\geq} := [\delta_{i \leq j}]_{i,j=1}^K \qquad
I_{<} := [\delta_{i > j}]_{i,j=1}^K \qquad
I_{\ll} := [\delta_{i > j+1}]_{i,j=1}^K
\end{align*}
so that for example, $\mathbf{1} \circ A = A$, $I_{\leq} \circ A$ extracts the lower triangular matrix 
of $A$ (including the diagonal), and $I_{\ll} \circ A = 0$ enforces Hessenberg form.

\subsection{Sparsity notation}\label{subsec:sparsity_notation}
It will be convenient to characterize the sparsity and dimensionality of vectors and matrices succinctly. We use the symbol $*$ to denote an unknown scalar. Thus, $(*, 0, 0)^\top$ can be thought of as the span of the first unit vector. Formally, define an extension of the reals as $\mathbb{R}_* = \mathbb{R} \cup \{*\}$, where the sum and product with $*$ are extended as 
\begin{align*}
    s + * = * \quad\quad\quad
    s \cdot * = \bigg\{ \begin{array}{cr} 0 & s = 0 \\ * & \text{otherwise}  \end{array}
\end{align*}
Note that this implies $* - * = *$. Now, for $s, s' \in \mathbb{R}_*$, define partial order $$s \preceq s' \iff \square(s) \subseteq \square(s')$$ 
where
\begin{align*}
    % \square &: \mathbb{R}_* \to \{\mathbb{R}, \{r\} (r \in \mathbb{R})\}
    % \\
    \square(s) &= \bigg\{ \begin{array}{cr} \{s\} & s \in \mathbb{R} \\ \mathbb{R} & s = * \end{array}
\end{align*}
This implies $s \preceq *$. We extend $\mathbb{R}_*$ to vector spaces with an elementwise $+$, $\cdot$, and $\preceq$.

\subsection{Matrix free linear algebra}
Matrix-free linear algebra refers to implementing matrix operations without explicitly forming or storing the full matrices. Instead, we work with functions that compute matrix-vector products. This is particularly useful when dealing with large matrices where storing them would be impractical, or when the matrix is defined implicitly through some operation, where we never need the full matrix, only specific matrix-vector products.
For a linear operator $A: \mathbb{R}^m \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$, instead of storing an $n \times m$ matrix, we represent it as a function:

$$\text{matvec}: \mathbb{R}^m \times \Theta \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$$
such that $\text{matvec}(v, \theta) = A_\theta v$ for any vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^m$ and parameters $\theta \in \Theta$. This approach enables a general way to handle general linear operators, such as matrix-vector products with sparse and structured matrices, the jacobian-vector product of a neural network, finite-difference operators, discrete fourier transforms and other linear operators acting between vector spaces.

\subsection{Bidiagonalization}
$$\texttt{Bidiagonalize}_K: (A, v) \mapsto (L, R, B, r, c)$$
Bidiagonalizing an arbitrary matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times M}$ with initial vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^M$ and a prescribed number of iterations $K \in \mathbb{N}_+$, will yield column-orthonormal $L \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times K}$ and $R \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times K}$, bidiagonal $B \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}$, residual vector $r \in \mathbb{R}^M$ and inverse length $c \in \mathbb{R}$, satisfying the following constraints:
\begin{align}
    A^\top L &= RB^\top + r(e_K)^\top \label{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint1}
    \\
    AR &= LB \label{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint2}
    \\
    Re_1 &= cv \label{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint3}
    \\
    I_{\leq} \circ [L^\top L] &= I \label{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint4}
    \\
    I_{\leq} \circ [R^\top R] &= I \label{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint5}
    \\
    R^\top r &= 0 \label{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint6}
\end{align}
and $B$ is full rank and diagonally banded as follows:
\begin{align}
B = \underbrace{\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) + \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \text{k=1})}_{\text{B is 'upper bidiagonal'}} = \begin{bmatrix}
\alpha_1  & \beta_1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \alpha_2 & \beta_2 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
0 & 0 & \ddots & \ddots & 0 & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \alpha_{K-1}  & \beta_{K-1}\\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \alpha_K\\
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K} \label{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint7}
\end{align}
The full rank property on $B$ implies that no components of $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ or $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ can be zero.

\subsection{Hessenberg Factorization}
$$\texttt{Hessenberg}_K: (A, v) \mapsto (Q, H, r, c)$$
Given a square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ an initial vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^N$ and a prescribed number of iterations $K \in \mathbb{N}_+$, the Hessenberg factorization produces a column-orthogonal $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times K}$, 
structured $H \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}$, residual vector $r \in \mathbb{R}^N$, 
and reciprocal length $c \in \mathbb{R}$ such that
\begin{align}
AQ &= QH + r(e_K)^\top \label{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint1}
\\
Qe_1 &= cv \label{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint2}
\\
I_{\leq} \circ [Q^\top Q] &= I \label{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint3}
\\
I_{\ll} \circ H &= 0 \label{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint4}
\\
Q^\top r &= 0 \label{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint5}
\end{align}


\subsection{Hessenberg factorization generalizes Bidiagonalization}\label{sec:bidiag_through_hessenberg}
To compute $\texttt{Bidiagonalize}_K(A, v) = (L, R, B, r, c)$ for any $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times M}$ and $v \in \mathbb{R}^M$ using Hessenberg factorization, we construct the symmetric matrix $\dddot{A}$ and augmented vector $\dddot{v}$:
\begin{align}
    \dddot{A} := \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0} & A \\ A^\top & \mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{(N+M) \times (N+M)} \label{eq:A_sparsity_pattern}
\end{align}
    \begin{align}
\dddot{v} := \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0} \\ v \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{N+M} \label{eq:v_sparsity_pattern}
\end{align}
We define the operation $\texttt{augment}: (A, v) \mapsto (\dddot{A}, \dddot{v})$ as the augmentation of a matrix and vector to this symmetric matrix and padded vector.
Applying Hessenberg factorization for $2K$ iterations to this augmented input yields
$\texttt{Hessenberg}_{2K}(\dddot{A}, \dddot{v}) = (\dddot{Q}, \dddot{H}, \dddot{r}, c)$
which are directly related to $(L, R, B, r, c)$ through the following sparse equations:

\begin{align}
\dddot{Q} :&= \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0} & l_1 & \mathbf{0} & l_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{0} & l_k \\ r_1 & \mathbf{0} & r_2 & \mathbf{0} &  \cdots & r_k & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{(N+M) \times 2K}  \label{eq:Q_sparsity_pattern} 
\end{align}
\begin{align}
\dddot{r} &:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0} \\ r \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{N+M} \label{eq:r_sparsity_pattern}
\end{align}
\begin{align}
\dddot{H} := \begin{bmatrix}
0 & \alpha_1 & 0  & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
\alpha_1 & 0  & \beta_1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
0 & \beta_1  & 0 & \alpha_2 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
0 & 0 & \alpha_2 & 0  & \beta_2 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0 & \beta_2  & 0 & \ddots & 0 & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0   & \ddots & 0 & \beta_{K-1} & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \beta_{K-1}  & 0 & \alpha_K\\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0  & \alpha_K & 0\\
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2K \times 2K} \label{eq:H_sparsity_pattern}
\end{align}
The vectors $l_i$ and $r_i$ in $\dddot{Q}$ are exactly the columns of $L$ and $R$. $\dddot{H}$ is symmetric and banded, where the two bands hold the entries of the bidiagonal matrix $B$ with twice repeated interwoven $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ and $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ values. Recall that $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ and $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ were everywhere nonzero. The residual vector $\dddot{r}$ is a padded version of $r$, and the inverse norm $c$ is unchanged.
\\
We formalize this relationship through a linear extraction mapping 
$$\texttt{extract}: (\dddot{Q}, \dddot{H}, \dddot{r}, c) \mapsto (L, R, B, r, c)$$ 
that just projects away the zeros and extracts the relevant non-zero entries from the Hessenberg outputs.\footnote{The projection from $\dddot{H}$ to $B$ is not unique, as $\dddot{H}$ contains two copies of each $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$. We arbitrarily select the superdiagonal entries and drop the subdiagonal entries. This choice affects the sparsity pattern of $\nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho$ in section \ref{subsec:derivation_stable_adjoint_system}. Any other convex linear combination of each pair would also be valid.} This allows us to formalize \textbf{augmented Bidiagonalization} as $$\overline{\texttt{Bidiagonalize}}_K := \texttt{extract} \circ \texttt{Hessenberg}_{2K} \circ \texttt{augment} : (A, v) \mapsto (L, R, B, r, c)$$ which is functionally equivalent to standard Bidiagonalization but inherits the numerical stability properties of Hessenberg factorization's adjoint system. 
\\
The problem is that this is "computing a lot of zeros". We will show that we can prune the computational graph to minimize work.

\subsection{The adjoint method}\label{subsec:adjoint_method}
The adjoint method computes parameter gradients of losses that depend on the output of a parameterized numerical solver. Let $s : \theta \mapsto x$ be a solver that yields a solution $x$ to a problem $\theta$, with $\text{dim}(\theta) = P$ and $\text{dim}(x) = X$. Suppose we know a constraint function $c : x \mapsto \mathbb{R}^X$ with $c(s(\theta), \theta) = \mathbf{0}$ for all $\theta$ and with $\frac{\partial c}{\partial \theta}$ everywhere nonsingular, and a loss $\ell : x \mapsto \mathbb{R}$. We seek $\nabla_\theta \ell(s(\theta))$.
\begin{example}[Hessenberg Factorization]
    For $s = $ \texttt{Hessenberg} we have $\theta = (A, v)$ and $x = (Q, H, r, c)$. The constraint function is the concatenation of constraints \eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint1} through \eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint5}, so $c(x, \theta) = \mathbf{0}$ exactly when $(Q, H, r, c)$ correctly Hessenberg-factorizes $(A, v)$.
\end{example}
Because $c(s(\theta), \theta) = \mathbf{0}$ is constantly zero, its total derivative is also zero, so the chain rule gives
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{0} = \frac{d}{d\theta} c(s(\theta), \theta) = \frac{\partial c}{\partial s} \frac{\partial s}{\partial \theta} + \frac{\partial c}{\partial \theta}
    \implies \frac{\partial c}{\partial s} \frac{\partial s}{\partial \theta} = -\frac{\partial c}{\partial \theta} \label{eq:exploit_constraint}
\end{align}
Applying the chain rule to the gradient itself,
\begin{align}
    \nabla_\theta \ell(s(\theta)) = \frac{\partial s}{\partial \theta}^\top\nabla_x \ell(s(\theta))  \label{eq:gradient_chain_rule}
\end{align}
we assume $\nabla_x \ell(s(\theta))$ is easy to obtain. The challenge is $\frac{\partial s}{\partial \theta} \in \mathbb{R}^{X \times P}$, which is expensive for high-dimensional $x$. If we can find \textit{adjoint variables} $\boldsymbol{\lambda} \in \mathbb{R}^X$ solving the uniquely determined $X \times X$ linear system
\begin{align}
    \nabla_x \ell(s(\theta)) = \frac{\partial c}{\partial s}^\top \boldsymbol{\lambda}  \label{eq:adjoint_system}
\end{align}
then substituting into \eqref{eq:gradient_chain_rule} yields
\begin{align*}
    \nabla_\theta \ell(s(\theta)) = \frac{\partial s}{\partial \theta}^\top \frac{\partial c}{\partial s}^\top \boldsymbol{\lambda} = \left(\frac{\partial c}{\partial s} \frac{\partial s}{\partial \theta}\right)^\top \boldsymbol{\lambda} \overset{\eqref{eq:exploit_constraint}}{=} -\frac{\partial c}{\partial \theta}^\top \boldsymbol{\lambda}
\end{align*}
This is much cheaper than the direct approach. Computing $\frac{\partial s}{\partial \theta}$ directly costs $O(PX^3)$ operations (one $X \times X$ solve per parameter), whereas the adjoint approach solves a single $X \times X$ system \eqref{eq:adjoint_system} and evaluates $-\frac{\partial c}{\partial \theta}^\top \boldsymbol{\lambda}$, for $O(X^3 + XP)$ operations. When $P \ll X$, this is the difference between $O(X^3)$ and $O(PX^3)$, which is decisive in applications like neural network training or PDE-constrained optimization where $X$ is very large.

\section{The adjoint system of Hessenberg factorization}

\subsection{The adjoint constraints}\label{subsec:adjoint_hessenberg}
Let $K \in \mathbb{N}_+$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times N}$, $v \in \mathbb{R}^N$, $H \in \mathbb{R}^{K\times K}$, $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times K}$, $r \in \mathbb{R}^N$, $c \in \mathbb{R}$ satisfy the primal constraints \eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint1} through \eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint5}. Let adjoint variables $\Lambda \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times K}$ with $\Lambda e_i = \lambda_i$, $\Gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{K\times K}$, $\lambda_0 \in \mathbb{R}^N$, $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}^K$ solve the following system of equations:
\begin{align}
0 &= \nabla_{Q} \rho + A^\top \Lambda - \Lambda H^\top + \lambda_0 (e_{1})^\top 
   + Q(I_{\leq} \circ \Gamma) + Q(I_{\leq} \circ \Gamma)^\top + r \gamma^\top \label{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint1}\\ 
0 &= \nabla_{H} \rho - Q^\top \Lambda + I_{\ll} \circ \mathcal{S} \label{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint2}\\
0 &= \nabla_{r} \rho - \Lambda e_{K} + Q \gamma \label{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint3}\\
0 &= \nabla_{c} \rho - v^\top \lambda_0 \label{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint4}
\end{align}
The gradients $\nabla_A\rho$ and $\nabla_v\rho$ are then given by\footnote{Note a sign difference between \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint6} and the corresponding result of \citet{kraemer2024gradients}, which is due to a typo there.}:
\begin{align}
    \nabla_A\rho &= \Lambda Q^\top \label{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint5} \\
    \nabla_v\rho &= -c\lambda_0 \label{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint6}
\end{align}


\subsection{Solving the adjoint system}\label{subsec:solving_adjoint_system}
The output gradients $\nabla_Q\rho$, $\nabla_H\rho$, $\nabla_r\rho$, $\nabla_c\rho$ will have know quantities, so we need only determine the adjoint variables $\Lambda$, $\Gamma$, $\lambda$, $\gamma$. $\Gamma$ is involved only in equation \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint1} in which only the lower triangular part is accessed, so for notational convenience we will redefine $\Gamma$ to be lower triangular, meaning we can effectively drop the mask $\Gamma := I_{\leq} \circ \Gamma$.
\\
We get expressions for $\lambda_K$ and $\gamma$:
\begin{align}
    \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint3} & \iff & \lambda_K &= \nabla_r \rho + Q \gamma \label{eq:get_last_lambda}
    \\
    & \;\overset{\eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint3}}{\implies} &Q^\top \lambda_K &= Q^\top \nabla_r \rho + Q^\top Q \gamma \nonumber
    \\ 
    &&&= Q^\top \nabla_r \rho + \gamma \nonumber
    \\ 
    & \iff & \gamma &= Q^\top \lambda_K - Q^\top \nabla_r \rho \label{eq:get_gamma_almost}
\end{align}
To evaluate $Q^\top \lambda_k$, inspect \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint2}:
\begin{align}
    \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint2} & \iff &Q^\top \Lambda &= \nabla_{H} \rho + I_{\ll} \circ \mathcal{S} \nonumber
    \\
    & \;\implies & (\mathbf{1} - I_{\ll}) \circ Q^\top \Lambda &= (\mathbf{1} - I_{\ll}) \circ \nabla_{H} \rho + (\mathbf{1} - I_{\ll}) \circ I_{\ll} \circ \Sigma \nonumber
    \\ 
    &&&= (\mathbf{1} - I_{\ll}) \circ \nabla_{H} \rho \label{eq:get_lambda_in_Q_coords}
    \\
    & \;\implies & Q^\top \lambda_K &= \nabla_{H} \rho e_K \nonumber
    \\
    &\;\overset{\eqref{eq:get_gamma_almost}}{\implies} &  \gamma &= \nabla_{H} \rho e_K - Q^\top \nabla_r \rho \label{eq:get_gamma}
\end{align}
This final relation lets us express $\gamma$ in terms of the known quantities, which we can then plug into \eqref{eq:get_last_lambda} to get $\lambda_K$.
\\
Next, we will get expressions for the remaining columns of $\Lambda$ and $\Gamma$, as well as for $\lambda$. As a preliminary, from primal constraints \eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint2} we have $v = q_1/c$, yielding 
\begin{align}
    \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint4} \iff c\nabla_{c} \rho = q_1^\top \lambda\label{eq:get_q1_top_lambda}
\end{align}
We now turn to adjoint system constraint \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint1}, left-multiply $Q^\top$ and then apply a mask to consider only the upper triangular part of the equations:
\begin{align*}
    \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint1} &\overset{\eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint3}, \eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint5}}{\implies} \empty & \mathbf{0} &= Q^\top \nabla_{Q} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \Lambda - Q^\top \Lambda H^\top + Q^\top \lambda_0 (e_{1})^\top + \Gamma + \Gamma^\top
    \\
    \empty &\;\implies\; \empty & \mathbf{0} &= I_\geq \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{Q} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \Lambda - Q^\top \Lambda H^\top + Q^\top \lambda_0 (e_{1})^\top + \Gamma + \Gamma^\top\bigg]
    \\
    \empty & \empty & &= I_\geq \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{Q} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \Lambda - Q^\top \Lambda H^\top + Q^\top \lambda_0 (e_{1})^\top\bigg] + \text{diag}(\Gamma) + \Gamma^\top
\end{align*}
We now inspect column $i$ in this matrix equation. We will use sparsity of $H$ to rewrite the matrix product $Q^\top \Lambda H^\top$, and we will apply some simplifications with the mask $I_\geq$:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{0} &= \left(I_\geq \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{Q} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \Lambda - Q^\top \Lambda H^\top + Q^\top \lambda_0 (e_{1})^\top\bigg]\right)e_i + \left(\text{diag}(\Gamma) + \Gamma^\top\right) e_i \nonumber
    \\
    &= I_\geq e_i \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{q_i} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \lambda_i - Q^\top \Lambda H^\top e_i + Q^\top \lambda_0 (e_{1})^\top e_i\bigg] + \text{diag}(\Gamma)e_i + \Gamma^\top e_i \nonumber
    \\
    &= I_\geq e_i \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{q_i} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \lambda_i - Q^\top \left(\sum_{j=i-1}^{K} \lambda_j h_{ij}\right)\bigg] +(I_\geq e_i) \circ Q^\top \lambda_0 \delta_{i=1} + \Gamma_{ii}e_i + \Gamma^\top e_i \nonumber
    \\
    &= I_\geq e_i \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{q_i} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \lambda_i - \sum_{j=i-1}^{K} h_{ij}Q^\top\lambda_j\bigg] + e_1q_1^\top \lambda_0 \delta_{i=1} + \Gamma_{ii}e_i + \Gamma^\top e_i \nonumber
    \\
    &\!\overset{\eqref{eq:get_q1_top_lambda}}{=} I_\geq e_i \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{q_i} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \lambda_i\bigg] - I_\geq e_i \circ \left(\sum_{j=i-1}^{K} h_{ij}Q^\top\lambda_j\right) + e_1c\nabla_{c} \rho \delta_{i=1} + \Gamma_{ii}e_i + \Gamma^\top e_i \nonumber
    \\
    &= I_\geq e_i \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{q_i} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \lambda_i\bigg] - \left(\sum_{j=i-1}^{K} h_{ij} I_\geq e_i \circ Q^\top\lambda_j\right) + e_1c\nabla_{c} \rho \delta_{i=1} + \Gamma_{ii}e_i + \Gamma^\top e_i \label{eq:term_round_parentheses}
\end{align}
We can use \eqref{eq:get_lambda_in_Q_coords} to rewrite the term in round parentheses:
\begin{align}
    &\eqref{eq:get_lambda_in_Q_coords}:& (\mathbf{1} - I_{\ll}) \circ Q^\top \Lambda &= (\mathbf{1} - I_{\ll}) \circ \nabla_{H} \rho \nonumber &
    \\
    &\;\implies &  (\mathbf{1} - I_{<}) \circ Q^\top \Lambda &= (\mathbf{1} - I_{<}) \circ \nabla_{H} \rho \nonumber &
    \\
    &\iff &  I_{\geq} \circ Q^\top \Lambda &= I_{\geq} \circ \nabla_{H} \rho \nonumber &
    \\
    &\;\implies &  I_{\geq}e_i \circ Q^\top \lambda_i &= I_{\geq}e_i \circ \nabla_{H} \rho e_i \nonumber &
\end{align}
We plug this into \eqref{eq:term_round_parentheses}, which then yields an expression we can use to solve for a column $i$ of $\Gamma^\top$, requiring knowledge only of the value of $\lambda_i$:
\begin{align}
    \Gamma_{ii}e_i + \Gamma^\top e_i = - I_\geq e_i \circ \bigg[Q^\top \nabla_{q_i} \rho + Q^\top A^\top \lambda_i\bigg] + \left(\sum_{j=i-1}^{K} h_{ij} I_\geq e_i \circ \nabla_{H} \rho e_j\right) - e_1c\nabla_{c} \rho \delta_{i=1} \label{eq:get_Gamma_i}
\end{align}
\\
Finally, to solve for $\lambda_i$ and $\lambda$, we look again at \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint1} and restrict to column $i$:
\begin{align}
    \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint1} \implies & \empty & \!\!0 &\!= \!\nabla_{\!q_i} \rho + A^\top \!\lambda_i - \!\Lambda H^{\!\top}\!e_i + \lambda_0 \delta_{i=1} 
    + Q\Gamma e_i + Q\Gamma^\top e_i + r \gamma^\top e_i \nonumber
    \\
    &\empty &\empty &\!= \!\nabla_{q_i} \rho + A^\top \lambda_i - \!\sum_{j=i-1}^{K} h_{ij} \lambda_j + \lambda_0 \delta_{i=1} + Q(\Gamma + \Gamma^\top)e_i + r \gamma^\top e_i \nonumber
    \\
    \iff&  & \!\!\!\!\!\begin{array}{cc}
        - \lambda_0 & \!\!i = 1 \\
      h_{i, i\!-\!1} \lambda_{i\!-\!1} & \!\!i > 1
   \end{array}\!\!\bigg\} &\!= \!\nabla_{q_i} \rho + A^\top \lambda_i - \!\sum_{j=i}^{K} h_{ij} \lambda_j + Q(\Gamma + \Gamma^\top)e_i + r \gamma^\top e_i \label{eq:get_lambda_i}
\end{align}
We now have all equations necessary to solve for all adjoint variables. There is a part that we have not yet addressed: In \eqref{eq:get_lambda_i}, we will need access to $(\Gamma + \Gamma^\top)e_i$, however \eqref{eq:get_Gamma_i} will only give us $\Gamma^\top e_i$. How we do this is best explained with an example.

\section{A stable adjoint system for Bidiagonalization}

\subsection{Deriving the adjoint system} \label{subsec:derivation_stable_adjoint_system}
As described in \ref{sec:bidiag_through_hessenberg}, we can use $\overline{\texttt{Bidiagonalize}}_K$ to bidiagonalize any matrix $A$ with initial vector $v$. The detour through $\texttt{Hessenberg}$ lets us use the adjoint system of Hessenberg Factorization described in \ref{subsec:adjoint_hessenberg}. In this section we will inspect the adjoint system of $\overline{\texttt{Bidiagonalize}}_K$, and simplify it considerably by exploiting sparsity in $\dddot{A}, \dddot{v}, \dddot{Q}, \dddot{H}$ and $\dddot{r}$.
\\[6pt]
\noindent\textbf{Overview of the argument.}
The derivation proceeds in four stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The Hessenberg adjoint system (section \ref{subsec:adjoint_hessenberg}) provides recurrence relations \eqref{eq:get_gamma}, \eqref{eq:get_last_lambda}, \eqref{eq:get_Gamma_i}, \eqref{eq:get_lambda_i} for the adjoint variables $\Lambda, \Gamma, \lambda_0, \gamma$.
    \item We state and prove a sparsity theorem (sections \ref{subsec:sparsity_gamma_lambda2K}--\ref{subsec:sparsity_Gamma_Lambda}): when these recurrences are instantiated on the augmented system $(\dddot{A}, \dddot{Q}, \dddot{H}, \dddot{r})$, the adjoint variables inherit an alternating block-zero structure.
    \item The sparsity allows decomposing each $(N{+}M)$-dimensional adjoint vector into an $N$-part ($\phi_i$) and an $M$-part ($\xi_i$), and the $2K \times 2K$ matrix $\Gamma$ into two $K \times K$ lower-triangular matrices $\Omega$ and $\Sigma$.
    \item Substituting these decompositions back into the Hessenberg recurrences and simplifying yields the compact stable bidiag adjoint system (section \ref{subsec:stable_adjoint_system}), which operates entirely in the original $N, M, K$ dimensions.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent We will look at the constraints \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint1} through \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint4} and determine further sparsity patterns in the adjoint variables $\Lambda, \Gamma, \lambda, \gamma$. The following theorem summarizes the findings:
\begin{theorem}
    Let loss $\rho^\star(L, R, B, r, c) \in \mathbb{R}$ be a loss of the output of Bidiagonalization. Let $(A, v) := \texttt{augment}(A', v')$. Then, the adjoint variables $\Lambda \in \mathbb{R}^{(N+M) \times 2K}, \Gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{2K \times 2K}, \lambda_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{N+M}, \gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{2K} $ associated with a loss 
    $$\rho(A, v) := (\rho^\star \circ \texttt{extract} \circ \texttt{Hessenberg}_{2K})(A, v) \in \mathbb{R}$$ associated with are highly sparse. In addition to the constraints of the adjoint system given in \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint1} - \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint4}, for $i \in \{1, \dots, 2K\}$, they satisfy the sparsity relations
    $$\begin{array}{rrl}
        \text{For even $i$: }& \quad\quad \Lambda e_i \preceq& \big[\overbrace{0 \;\; 0 \;\; \cdots \;\; 0}^{N} \;\; \overbrace{* \;\; * \;\; \cdots \;\; *}^{M}\big] ^\top
        \\
        \text{For odd $i$: }& \quad\quad \Lambda e_i \preceq& \big[\overbrace{* \;\; * \;\; \cdots \;\; *}^{N} \; \;\overbrace{0 \;\; 0 \;\; \cdots \;\; 0}^{M}\big]^\top 
        \\
        \\
        \text{For even $i$: }& \quad\quad \Gamma^\top e_i \preceq& I_{\geq}e_i \circ \begin{bmatrix} * & 0 & * & 0 &  \cdots & 0 & * & 0\end{bmatrix} ^\top \vspace{10px}
        \\
        \text{For odd $i$: }&  \quad \quad \Gamma^\top e_i \preceq& I_{\geq}e_i \circ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & * & 0 & * & \cdots & * & 0 & *\end{bmatrix} ^\top
    \end{array}$$
    $$\lambda_0 \preceq \big[\overbrace{0 \;\; 0 \;\; \cdots \;\; 0}^{N} \;\; \overbrace{* \;\; * \;\; \cdots \;\; *}^{M}\big] ^\top \quad \quad \gamma \preceq \begin{bmatrix} * & 0 & * & 0 & \cdots & 0 & * & 0 \end{bmatrix}^\top$$
\end{theorem}
\noindent
\\
We will give names to all nonzero entries in $\Lambda, \Gamma, \lambda, \gamma$.
The theorem implies that $\Gamma$ is lower triangular and has a checkered sparsity pattern with a zero in the lower left. The structure is best conveyed for $K=6$, whose entries we name in the following systematic manner
$$\Gamma = \begin{bmatrix}
    \omega_{11} & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & \sigma_{11} & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
    \omega_{21} & 0 & \omega_{22} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & \sigma_{21} & 0 & \sigma_{22} & 0 & 0 \\
    \omega_{31} & 0 & \omega_{32} & 0 & \omega_{33} & 0 \\
    0 & \sigma_{31} & 0 & \sigma_{32} & 0 & \sigma_{33} \\
\end{bmatrix} \begin{array}{c}\text{ with }\\\text{ aliases }\end{array} \begin{array}{c}\Omega := \begin{bmatrix}
    \omega_{11} & 0 & 0\\
    \omega_{21} & \omega_{22} & 0\\
    \omega_{31} & \omega_{32} & \omega_{33}\\
\end{bmatrix} \\ \\ \Sigma := \begin{bmatrix}
    \sigma_{11} & 0 & 0 \\
    \sigma_{21} & \sigma_{22} & 0 \\
    \sigma_{31} & \sigma_{32} & \sigma_{33} \\
\end{bmatrix}\end{array}$$
where the defined of $\Omega$ and $\Sigma$ will turn out to be convenient later. The theorem also states that $\Lambda$ has pattern:
$$\Lambda = \begin{bmatrix}
    \phi_1 & \mathbf{0} & \phi_2 & \mathbf{0} & \cdots & \phi_k &  \mathbf{0}\\ 
    \mathbf{0} & \xi_1 & \mathbf{0} & \xi_2 &  \cdots & \mathbf{0} & \xi_k
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{(N+M) \times 2K} \begin{array}{c}\text{ with }\\\text{ aliases }\end{array}  \begin{array}{c} \Phi := [\phi_1 \cdots \phi_k] \\ \Xi := [\xi_1 \cdots \xi_k] \end{array}
$$
and the entries of $\lambda_0$ and $\gamma$ as 
$$\lambda_0 = \begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{0} & \xi_0
\end{bmatrix}^\top \qquad \gamma = \begin{bmatrix}
    \eta_1 & 0 & \eta_2 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & \eta_K & 0
\end{bmatrix}^\top \begin{array}{c}\text{ with }\\\text{ alias }\end{array} \; \eta := \begin{pmatrix}\eta_1 \\ \vdots \\ \eta_K\end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^K$$

\subsubsection{Sparsity patterns of the input gradients.}
The sparsity patterns of $\nabla_{\dddot{Q}} \rho$, $\nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho$, and $\nabla_{\dddot{r}} \rho$ match those of $\dddot{Q}, \dddot{H}, \dddot{r}$, due to the linear nature of \texttt{extract}:
\begin{align}
    \nabla_{\dddot{Q}} \rho := \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0} & \nabla_{l_1}\rho & \mathbf{0} & \nabla_{l_2}\rho & \cdots & \mathbf{0} & \nabla_{l_k}\rho \\ \nabla_{r_1}\rho & \mathbf{0} & \nabla_{r_2}\rho & \mathbf{0} &  \cdots & \nabla_{r_k}\rho & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{(N+M) \times 2K} \label{eq:nabla_Q_sparsity_pattern}
\end{align}
\begin{align}
    \nabla_{\dddot{r}} \rho := \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0} \\ \nabla_{r} \rho \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{N+M} \label{eq:nabla_r_sparsity_pattern}
\end{align}
\begin{align}
    \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho := \begin{bmatrix} 
    0 & \nabla_{\alpha_1}\rho & 0  & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
    0 & 0  & \nabla_{\beta_1}\rho & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
    0 & 0  & 0 & \nabla_{\alpha_2}\rho & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \nabla_{\beta_2}\rho & 0 & 0 & 0\\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \ddots & 0 & 0\\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \nabla_{\beta_{K-1}}\rho & 0\\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0  & 0 & \nabla_{\alpha_K}\rho\\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0  & 0 & 0\\
    \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2K \times 2K} \label{eq:nabla_H_sparsity_pattern}
\end{align}
The proof of this theorem is a backward induction on the sparsity patterns; we defer it to Appendix~\ref{app:sparsity_proofs} and proceed to use the result.

\subsection{The stable adjoint system}\label{subsec:stable_adjoint_system}
Having established the sparsity of all adjoint variables, we now substitute the decompositions from the theorem back into the Hessenberg recurrences \eqref{eq:get_gamma}, \eqref{eq:get_last_lambda}, \eqref{eq:get_Gamma_i}, and \eqref{eq:get_lambda_i}. Each equation separates into independent $N$-block and $M$-block components due to the block-diagonal/block-off-diagonal structure of the augmented system. We treat each recurrence in turn.

\subsubsection{Reducing the initialization}
Recall from \eqref{eq:get_gamma} that $\gamma = \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho\, e_{2K} - \dddot{Q}^\top \nabla_{\dddot{r}} \rho$. We have already computed in section~\ref{subsec:sparsity_gamma_lambda2K} that
\begin{align*}
    \gamma &= \begin{pmatrix}0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & \nabla_{\alpha_K} \rho & 0\end{pmatrix}^\top
    - 
    \begin{pmatrix}r_1^\top \nabla_r\rho & 0 & r_2^\top \nabla_r\rho & 0 & \cdots & r_K^\top \nabla_r\rho & 0\end{pmatrix}^\top
\end{align*}
Extracting the odd-indexed entries gives $\eta \in \mathbb{R}^K$ with
\begin{align}
    \eta_n &= -r_n^\top \nabla_r\rho \text{\quad for $n < K$}, \qquad \eta_K = \nabla_{\alpha_K}\rho - r_K^\top \nabla_r\rho \label{eq:stable_eta}
\end{align}
or equivalently $\eta = -R^\top \nabla_r \rho + \nabla_{\alpha_K}\rho\, e_K$.
Similarly, from \eqref{eq:get_last_lambda}, $\lambda_{2K} = \nabla_{\dddot{r}} \rho + \dddot{Q} \gamma$. The $M$-block (extracting the lower half) gives
\begin{align}
    \xi_K &= \nabla_r \rho + R\eta \label{eq:stable_xi_K}
\end{align}
where we used that $\dddot{Q}\gamma$ restricted to the $M$-block is $\sum_{n=1}^K r_n \eta_n = R\eta$.

\subsubsection{Reducing the reprojection constraints}
The key identity from section~\ref{subsec:solving_adjoint_system} is \eqref{eq:get_lambda_in_Q_coords}: $I_{\geq} \circ \dddot{Q}^\top \Lambda = I_{\geq} \circ \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho$.
Using the block structures of $\dddot{Q}$ and $\Lambda$:
\begin{align*}
    \dddot{Q}^\top \Lambda &= \begin{bmatrix}
    0 & l_1^\top \phi_1 & 0 & l_1^\top \phi_2 & \cdots \\
    r_1^\top \xi_1 & 0 & r_1^\top \xi_2 & 0 & \cdots \\
    0 & l_2^\top \phi_1 & 0 & l_2^\top \phi_2 & \cdots \\
    r_2^\top \xi_1 & 0 & r_2^\top \xi_2 & 0 & \cdots \\
    \vdots & & & & \ddots
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}
The odd-odd block of $I_\geq \circ \dddot{Q}^\top \Lambda$ (rows $2n{-}1$, columns $2m{-}1$) contains $r_n^\top \xi_m$ for $n \leq m$, and the even-even block (rows $2n$, columns $2m$) contains $l_n^\top \phi_m$ for $n \leq m$.
Matching with $I_\geq \circ \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho$, whose even-even block has $\nabla_{\beta_n}\rho$ at position $(n, n{+}1)$ and zeros elsewhere, and whose odd-odd block has $\nabla_{\alpha_n}\rho$ at position $(n, n)$ and zeros elsewhere, we obtain the reprojection constraints:
\begin{align}
    I_{\geq} \circ (L^\top \Phi) &= \text{diag}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\rho,\; k\!=\!1) \label{eq:stable_reprojection_L}
    \\
    I_{\geq+1} \circ (R^\top \Xi) &= \text{diag}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\rho) \label{eq:stable_reprojection_R}
\end{align}
where $I_{\geq+1}$ denotes the mask with $[\delta_{i \leq j+1}]_{i,j}$, reflecting the one-index offset between the $R$ and $L$ constraints. The remaining entries of the $\Gamma$ recurrence \eqref{eq:get_Gamma_i} build up $\Sigma + \Sigma^\top$ and $\Omega + \Omega^\top$ column by column, following the same backward loop as in the general Hessenberg case (see Example~\ref{ex:construction_Gamma_Gamma_top}), but now restricted to the $K \times K$ even-even and odd-odd blocks independently.

\subsubsection{Reducing the $\Lambda$ recurrence}
The recurrence \eqref{eq:get_lambda_i} reads $h_{i,i-1}\lambda_{i-1} = \nabla_{\dddot{q}_i}\rho + \dddot{A}^\top \lambda_i - \sum_{j=i}^{2K} h_{ij}\lambda_j + \dddot{Q}(\Gamma + \Gamma^\top)e_i + \dddot{r}\,\gamma^\top e_i$ for $i > 1$, and $-\lambda_0 = (\cdots)$ for $i=1$.
\\[4pt]
\noindent\textit{Even $i = 2n$.} The LHS is $\alpha_n \lambda_{2n-1}$, whose nonzero part is the $N$-block $\alpha_n \phi_n$. Extracting the $N$-block of each RHS term:
\begin{align*}
    \nabla_{\dddot{q}_{2n}} \rho &\to \nabla_{l_n}\rho
    &
    \dddot{A}^\top \lambda_{2n} &\to A\xi_n
    \\
    h_{2n,2n+1}\lambda_{2n+1} &\to \beta_n \phi_{n+1}
    &
    \dddot{Q}(\Gamma + \Gamma^\top)e_{2n} &\to L(\Sigma + \Sigma^\top) e_n
\end{align*}
The term $\dddot{r}\,\gamma^\top e_{2n} = \mathbf{0}$ since $\gamma$ is zero in even entries. Dividing by $\alpha_n$ and collecting all columns:
\begin{align}
    0 &= \nabla_L + A\Xi - \Phi B^\top + L(\Sigma + \Sigma^\top) \label{eq:stable_Phi_recurrence}
\end{align}
\noindent\textit{Odd $i = 2n{-}1$.} The LHS is $\beta_{n-1}\lambda_{2n-2}$ (for $n > 1$) or $-\lambda_0$ (for $n = 1$), whose nonzero part is the $M$-block: $\beta_{n-1}\xi_{n-1}$ or $-\xi_0 = -\kappa$. Extracting the $M$-block of each RHS term:
\begin{align*}
    \nabla_{\dddot{q}_{2n-1}} \rho &\to \nabla_{r_n}\rho
    &
    \dddot{A}^\top \lambda_{2n-1} &\to A^\top \phi_n
    \\
    h_{2n-1,2n}\lambda_{2n} &\to \alpha_n \xi_n
    &
    \dddot{Q}(\Gamma + \Gamma^\top)e_{2n-1} &\to R(\Omega + \Omega^\top) e_n
\end{align*}
The term $\dddot{r}\,\gamma^\top e_{2n-1} = \dddot{r}\,\eta_n$, whose $M$-block is $\text{res}\;\eta_n$ (using $\dddot{r} = [\mathbf{0};\, \text{res}]$). The $n=1$ boundary contributes $\kappa\, e_1^\top$. Collecting:
\begin{align}
    0 &= \nabla_R + A^\top \Phi - \Xi B + R(\Omega + \Omega^\top) + \kappa\, e_1^\top + \text{res}\;\eta^\top \label{eq:stable_Xi_recurrence}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Reducing the gradient formulas}
From \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint5}, $\nabla_{\dddot{A}}\rho = \Lambda \dddot{Q}^\top$. This is an $(N{+}M) \times (N{+}M)$ matrix. Its $(1,2)$-block (the $N \times M$ part corresponding to $\nabla_A\rho$) is
\begin{align*}
    \nabla_A\rho &= \sum_{n=1}^K \phi_n \cdot r_n^\top + l_n \cdot \xi_n^\top
\end{align*}
which follows from the alternating structure: odd columns of $\Lambda$ contribute $\phi_n$ (in the $N$-block) paired with $r_n^\top$ (from odd rows of $\dddot{Q}^\top$), and even columns contribute $\xi_n$ paired with $l_n$. In compact form:
\begin{align}
    \nabla_A\rho &= \Phi R^\top + L\Xi^\top \label{eq:stable_grad_A}
\end{align}
From \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint6}, $\nabla_{\dddot{v}}\rho = -c\lambda_0 = -c\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0} \\ \kappa\end{pmatrix}$, so extracting the $M$-block:
\begin{align}
    \nabla_v\rho &= -c\kappa \label{eq:stable_grad_v}
\end{align}
From \eqref{eq:adjoint_hessenberg_constraint4}, $\nabla_c\rho = \dddot{v}^\top\lambda_0$. Using $\dddot{v} = \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0} \\ v\end{pmatrix}$ and $\lambda_0 = \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0} \\ \kappa\end{pmatrix}$:
\begin{align}
    \nabla_c\rho = v^\top \kappa = \frac{r_1^\top \kappa}{c} \qquad \iff \qquad c\nabla_c\rho = r_1^\top \kappa \label{eq:stable_grad_c}
\end{align}
where we used $r_1 = cv$ from the primal constraint \eqref{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint3}.

\subsubsection{Collected result}
Bringing together \eqref{eq:stable_eta}--\eqref{eq:stable_grad_c}, the stable adjoint system for Bidiagonalization is:
\begin{mdframed}
\begin{align}
    \eta &= -R^\top \nabla_r \rho + \nabla_{\alpha_K}\rho\, e_K \label{eq:stable_system_eta}
    \\
    \xi_K &= \nabla_r \rho + R\eta \label{eq:stable_system_xi_K}
    \\
    c\nabla_c\rho &= r_1^\top \kappa \label{eq:stable_system_c}
    \\
    0 &= \nabla_L + A\Xi - \Phi B^\top + L(\Sigma + \Sigma^\top) \label{eq:stable_system_Phi}
    \\
    0 &= \nabla_R + A^\top\!\Phi - \Xi B + R(\Omega + \Omega^\top) + \kappa\, e_1^\top + \text{res}\;\eta^\top \label{eq:stable_system_Xi}
    \\
    I_{\geq} \circ (L^\top \Phi) &= \text{diag}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\rho,\; k\!=\!1) \label{eq:stable_system_reprojL}
    \\
    I_{\geq+1} \circ (R^\top \Xi) &= \text{diag}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\rho) \label{eq:stable_system_reprojR}
    \\
    \nabla_v\rho &= -c\kappa \label{eq:stable_system_grad_v}
    \\
    \nabla_A\rho &= \Phi R^\top + L\Xi^\top \label{eq:stable_system_grad_A}
\end{align}
\end{mdframed}
\noindent This system is solved by the same backward loop as the general Hessenberg adjoint (section~\ref{subsec:solving_adjoint_system}), but operating entirely in the original dimensions: $\Phi, \Xi \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times K}$ and $\mathbb{R}^{M \times K}$ respectively, with $\Omega, \Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}$ lower triangular. The reprojection constraints \eqref{eq:stable_system_reprojL}--\eqref{eq:stable_system_reprojR} provide the numerical stability mechanism: they give an explicit target for $L^\top \phi_n$ and $R^\top \xi_n$ in known coordinates, enabling the reprojection correction described in section~\ref{sec:reprojection}.

\section{Numerical stability}\label{sec:stability}
Because Hessenberg factorization subsumes Bidiagonalization for the augmented input, the stability of the Hessenberg adjoint \citep{kraemer2024gradients} comes along with it. The mechanism is reprojection, which we describe next.

\subsection{Reprojection and reorthonormalization}\label{sec:reprojection}
Let $m$ be a mask vector with entries from $\{0, 1\}$. Let $L$ be an orthonormal matrix, and $q$ and $c$ be vectors. Consider a constraint of the form
$$m \circ (L^\top q) = m \circ c$$
that is, within the bounds of the mask $m$, the vector $q$ expressed in the basis of $L$ equals $c$.
Given a numerical estimate $q_0$ of a solution $q$, we get an improved estimate $q_1$ by \textit{reprojecting} it against $L$:
\begin{align*}
    q_0 &= q_0 - L (m \circ L^\top q_0) + L (m \circ L^\top q_0)
    \\
    &= q_0 - L (m \circ L^\top q_0) + L (m \circ c)
\end{align*}
which motivates the update step, repeated if necessary,
\begin{align*}
    q_1 &\leftarrow q_0 - L (m \circ L^\top q_0) + L (m \circ c)
\end{align*}
For Hessenberg factorization the relevant constraint is $Q^\top q_i = e_i$ \eqref{eq:hessenberg_forward_constraint3}, and the reprojection step followed by normalization is what we call \textit{reorthonormalization}. For Bidiagonalization, the analogous constraints are $L^\top l_i = e_i$ \eqref{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint4} and $R^\top r_i = e_i$ \eqref{eq:bidiag_forward_constraint5}. Empirically, two repetitions of the reprojection step are needed to eliminate numerical errors; with reprojection in place, the adjoint stays accurate across matrix sizes (Figure~\ref{fig:stability}). Figure~\ref{fig:reprojection} isolates this effect: as the problem size grows, the adjoint without reprojection drifts away from the identity Jacobian of matrix reconstruction, while the reprojection-corrected adjoint remains accurate.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{fig_reprojection.pdf}
    \caption{Loss of accuracy in the bidiagonalization adjoint. We bidiagonalize a dense random $n \times (n/2)$ matrix with $k=n/2$ steps, reconstruct $LBR^\top$, and measure the RMS error $\|\nabla_A(LBR^\top) - I\|_{\mathrm{rms}}$ (the Jacobian should be the identity). Without the reprojection step in the backward pass (solid red), accuracy degrades as $n$ grows; with reprojection (dashed green), it stays near machine precision.}
    \label{fig:reprojection}
\end{figure}

All methods are implemented in JAX~\citep{jax2018github}. We also compare against the augmented Hessenberg formulation. Unless stated otherwise, the matrix is dense and accessed through a diagonal matrix-vector product, computations use single precision, and timings are averaged over repeated runs after compilation. Compile time tells the simplest story (Figure~\ref{fig:compile}): the adjoint stays within a small factor of the forward pass, while autodiff keeps getting more expensive because it has to trace the whole unrolled iteration. Run time looks the same, with one extra wrinkle---autodiff eventually exhausts memory and stops, whereas the adjoint keeps going to much larger problems (Figure~\ref{fig:backward}). The accuracy gap is the widest of the three. As the matrix grows the adjoint tracks the true gradient to roughly seven orders of magnitude, while autodiff slowly drifts; and once the forward reorthonormalization itself is differentiated through, autodiff returns NaNs and is unusable (Figure~\ref{fig:stability}).

\section{Conclusion}
By viewing bidiagonalization as a Hessenberg factorization of an augmented symmetric matrix, we obtained a stable adjoint for it almost for free, and pruning the augmented system's zeros brought the cost back down to that of the original problem. The adjoint needs nothing beyond matrix-vector products and a reprojection step, and in our experiments it was the only one of the three methods that stayed both cheap and accurate at the larger sizes. The same augment-then-prune recipe should carry over to other structured factorizations that embed into a Hessenberg or symmetric Lanczos form; we leave that to future work.

\section{Sparsity proofs}\label{app:sparsity_proofs}

\subsection{Inspecting the sparsity patterns of $\gamma$ and $\lambda_{2K}$}\label{subsec:sparsity_gamma_lambda2K}
This section proves the statements $$\gamma \preceq \begin{bmatrix} * & 0 & * & 0 & \cdots & 0 & * & 0 \end{bmatrix}^\top$$ and $$\lambda_{2K} \preceq \big[\overbrace{0 \;\; 0 \;\; \cdots \;\; 0}^{N} \;\; \overbrace{* \;\; * \;\; \cdots \;\; *}^{M}\big] ^\top$$
Starting from \eqref{eq:get_gamma}, replacing $K$ with $2K$, we plug in our sparse gradients and sparse outputs:
\begin{align}
    \gamma &= \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_{2K} - \dddot{Q}^\top \nabla_{\dddot{r}} \rho = \nabla_{\alpha_{K}} \rho e_{2K-1} - \dddot{Q}^\top \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0} \\ \nabla_r\rho\end{pmatrix} \nonumber
    \\
    &= \begin{pmatrix}0 \!&\! 0 \!&\! 0 \!&\! 0 \!&\! \cdots \!&\! 0 \!&\! \nabla_{\alpha_K} \rho \!&\! 0\end{pmatrix}^\top
    - 
    \bigg[\nabla_{r} \rho ^\top \begin{pmatrix}r_1 \!&\! \mathbf{0} \!&\! r_2 \!&\! \mathbf{0} \!&\! \cdots \!&\!\mathbf{0}\!&\! r_K \!&\! \mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}\bigg]^\top \nonumber
    \\
    &\preceq 
    \begin{pmatrix}* \!&\! 0 \!&\! * \!&\! 0 \!&\! \cdots \!&\! 0 \!&\! * \!&\! 0\end{pmatrix}^\top \label{eq:gamma_sparsity_pattern}
\end{align}
This shows that $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{2K}$ is alternatingly non-zero and zero, meaning it is at most $K$-dimensional. We plug this pattern into \eqref{eq:get_last_lambda}:
\begin{align}
    \lambda_{2K} &= \nabla_{\dddot{r}} \rho + \dddot{Q} \gamma \nonumber
    \\ &\preceq \begin{pmatrix}0 \!&\! 0 \!&\! \cdots \!&\! 0 \!&\! * \!&\! * \!&\! \cdots \!&\! *\end{pmatrix}^\top + \dddot{Q} \begin{pmatrix}* \!&\! 0 \!&\! * \!&\! 0 \!&\! \cdots \!&\! 0\!&\! * \!&\! 0\end{pmatrix}^\top \nonumber
    \\
    &= \begin{pmatrix}0 \!&\! 0 \!&\! \cdots \!&\! 0 \!&\! * \!&\! * \!&\! \cdots \!&\! *\end{pmatrix}^\top \label{eq:lambda_2K_sparsity_pattern}
\end{align}
This shows that $\lambda_{2K} \in \mathbb{R}^{N+M}$ is $0$ in the leading $N$ entries, which also held true for the input vector $\dddot{v}$.
\subsection{Sparsity patterns of $\Gamma^\top$ and $\Lambda$}\label{subsec:sparsity_Gamma_Lambda}
This section proves the remainder of the theorem, namely that
$$\begin{array}{rrll}
    \text{For even $i$: }& \quad\quad \Bigg(\Lambda e_i \preceq& \big[\overbrace{0 \;\; 0 \;\; \cdots \;\; 0}^{N} \;\; \overbrace{* \;\; * \;\; \cdots \;\; *}^{M}\big] ^\top\Bigg) & =: P_\Lambda(i)
    \\
    \text{For odd $i$: }& \quad\quad \Bigg(\Lambda e_i \preceq& \big[\overbrace{* \;\; * \;\; \cdots \;\; *}^{N} \; \;\overbrace{0 \;\; 0 \;\; \cdots \;\; 0}^{M}\big]^\top \Bigg) & =: P_\Lambda(i)
    \\
    \\
    \text{For even $i$: }& \quad\quad \Bigg(\Gamma^\top e_i \preceq& I_{\geq}e_i \circ \begin{bmatrix} * & 0 & * & 0 &  \cdots & 0 & * & 0\end{bmatrix} ^\top\Bigg) & =: P_\Gamma(i)\vspace{10px}
    \\
    \text{For odd $i$: }&  \quad \quad \Bigg(\Gamma^\top e_i \preceq& I_{\geq}e_i \circ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & * & 0 & * & \cdots & * & 0 & *\end{bmatrix} ^\top\Bigg) & =: P_\Gamma(i)
\end{array}$$
where we have abbreviated the propositions as $P_\Lambda(i)$ and $P_\Gamma(i)$. 
$P_\Lambda(2K)$ is already proven in \eqref{eq:lambda_2K_sparsity_pattern}.
To prove the rest, we will use strong induction from $i = 2K$ down to $i=1$, by showing that
$$P_\Lambda(i) \!\!\implies P_\Gamma(i)$$
$$\bigwedge_{j=i}^{2K} \big(P_\Gamma(j) \big) \!\!\implies P_\Lambda(i-1)$$

The remaining equations that solve for the unknown adjoint variables $\Gamma^\top$ and $\Lambda$ are the recurrence relations \eqref{eq:get_Gamma_i} and \eqref{eq:get_lambda_i}.
To show these, we will use strong induction backwards, starting from $i=2K$ down to $i=1$, by assuming that it holds for the current and all preceding iterations $j \in \{i, i+1, \dots, 2K\}$. The base case $i = 2K$ is already proven for even $i=2K$ in \eqref{eq:lambda_2K_sparsity_pattern}. Assuming now that the hypothesis is true for some even iteration $i$ (and all earlier iterations) we will show that it holds for the preceding $i-1$ which is odd. To close the loop, we prove that, when it holds for an odd $i$ and earlier, it will hold for the preceding $i-1$, which is even.
\\

\subsection{Showing $P_\Lambda(i) \!\!\implies P_\Gamma(i)$}
We move onto equation \eqref{eq:get_Gamma_i} for columns of $\Gamma^\top$. We group it into four terms (I) - (IV) and analyze them separately, recombining at the end.
\begin{align}
    \Gamma_{ii}e_i + \Gamma^\top e_i &= - I_\geq e_i \circ \bigg[\underbrace{\dddot{Q}^\top \nabla_{\dddot{q_i}} \rho}_{(\text{I})} + \underbrace{\dddot{Q}^\top \dddot{A}^\top \lambda_i}_{(\text{II})}\bigg] + \left(\underbrace{\sum_{j=i-1}^{K} h_{ij} I_\geq e_i \circ \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_j}_{(\text{III})}\right) - \underbrace{e_1c\nabla_{c} \rho \delta_{i=1}}_{(\text{IV})}
\end{align}
We will expand the sparsity patterns of known variables $\dddot{Q}$, $\dddot{A}$, $\nabla_{\dddot{q_i}} \rho$, and $\nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho$, this will simplify.
\subsubsection{Term (I)}
$$(\text{I}) := \dddot{Q}^\top \nabla_{\dddot{q_{i}}} \rho$$
Using substitutions \eqref{eq:Q_sparsity_pattern} and \eqref{eq:nabla_Q_sparsity_pattern}, for even $i$:
\begin{align*}
    \dddot{Q}^\top \nabla_{\dddot{q_{i}}} \rho &= 
    \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0} & l_1 & \mathbf{0} & l_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{0} & l_k \\ r_1 & \mathbf{0} & r_2 & \mathbf{0} &  \cdots & r_k & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} ^\top \begin{bmatrix} \nabla_{l_i} \rho \\ \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} = 
    \begin{bmatrix} 0 & l_1^\top \nabla_{l_i} \rho & 0 & l_2^\top \nabla_{l_i} \rho & \cdots & 0 & l_k^\top \nabla_{l_i} \rho\end{bmatrix} ^\top
    \\
    &\preceq \begin{bmatrix} 0 & * & 0 & * & \cdots & 0 & * \end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}
For odd $i$: 
\begin{align*}
    \dddot{Q}^\top \nabla_{\dddot{q_{i}}} \rho &= 
    \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0} & l_1 & \mathbf{0} & l_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{0} & l_k \\ r_1 & \mathbf{0} & r_2 & \mathbf{0} &  \cdots & r_k & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} ^\top \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} \\ \nabla_{r_i} \rho \end{bmatrix} =
    \begin{bmatrix} r_1^\top \nabla_{r_i} \rho & 0 & r_2^\top \nabla_{r_i} \rho & 0 & \cdots & r_k^\top \nabla_{r_i} \rho & 0\end{bmatrix} ^\top
    \\
    &\preceq \begin{bmatrix} * & 0 & * & 0 & \cdots & * & 0 \end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}
\subsubsection{Term (II)}
$$(\text{II}) := \dddot{Q}^\top \dddot{A}^\top \lambda_i $$
Using again substitution \eqref{eq:Q_sparsity_pattern} in addition to \eqref{eq:A_sparsity_pattern}. We will also make use of the induction hypothesis on the sparsity of $\lambda_i$.
For even $i$:
\begin{align}
    \dddot{Q}^\top \dddot{A}^\top \lambda_i &= 
    \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0} & l_1 & \mathbf{0} & l_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{0} & l_k \\ r_1 & \mathbf{0} & r_2 & \mathbf{0} &  \cdots & r_k & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} ^\top \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} & A \\ A^\top & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} \\ \xi_i \end{bmatrix} = 
    \begin{bmatrix} 0 & l_1^\top A\xi_i & 0 & l_2^\top A\xi_i  & \cdots & 0 & l_k^\top A\xi_i \end{bmatrix} ^\top
    \\
    &\preceq \begin{bmatrix} 0 & * & 0 & * & \cdots & 0 & * \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}
For odd $i$, we get:
\begin{align}
    \dddot{Q}^\top \dddot{A}^\top \lambda_i &= 
    \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0} & l_1 & \mathbf{0} & l_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{0} & l_k \\ r_1 & \mathbf{0} & r_2 & \mathbf{0} &  \cdots & r_k & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} ^\top \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} & A \\ A^\top & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \phi_i \\ \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} = 
    \begin{bmatrix} r_1^\top A^\top \phi_i & 0 & r_2^\top A^\top \phi_i  & 0 & \cdots & r_k^\top A^\top \phi_i & 0 \end{bmatrix} ^\top
    \\
    &\preceq \begin{bmatrix} * & 0 & * & 0 & \cdots & * & 0 \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}
\subsubsection{Term (III)}
$$(\text{III}) := \sum_{j=i-1}^{K} h_{ij} I_\geq e_i \circ \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_j$$
Here, we make use of the sparsity pattern of $\nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho$ from \eqref{eq:nabla_H_sparsity_pattern}, which gives that, for even $i$,
$$\nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_i = \nabla_{\alpha_{i/2}} \rho e_{i-1}$$
and for odd $i$, 
$$\nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_i = \nabla_{\beta_{(i-1)/2}} \rho e_{i-1}$$
For this reason, all terms $I_\geq e_i \circ \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_j$ for $j \notin \{i-1, i, i+2\}$ are zero, so we can prune the sum. Additionally, we know from \eqref{eq:H_sparsity_pattern} that $h_{ii} = 0$ for all $i$.
Assuming now $i$ even:
\begin{align*}
    \sum_{j=i-1}^{K} h_{ij} I_\geq e_i \circ \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_j  
    &= 
    \sum_{j=i-1}^{i+1} h_{ij} I_\geq e_i \circ \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_j 
    \\ &= h_{i,i-1} \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_{i-1} + h_{i,i} \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_i + h_{i,i+1} \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_{i+1}
    \\ &= h_{i,i-1} \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_{i-1} + h_{i,i+1} \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_{i+1}
    \\ &= \alpha_{i/2} \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_{i-1} + \beta_{i/2} \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_{i+1}
    \\ &= \alpha_{i/2} \nabla_{\beta_{i/2 - 1}}\rho e_{i-2} + \beta_{i/2} \nabla_{\beta_{i/2}}\rho e_{i}
    \\ &\preceq * \cdot e_{i-2} + * \cdot e_{i}
\end{align*}
With a similar argument, for odd $i$:
\begin{align*}
    \sum_{j=i-1}^{K} h_{ij} I_\geq e_i \circ \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_j  
    &= h_{i,i-1} \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_{i-1} + h_{i,i+1} \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_{i+1}
    \\ &= \beta_{(i-1)/2} \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_{i-1} + \alpha_{(i+1)/2} \nabla_{\dddot{H}} \rho e_{i+1}
    \\ &= \beta_{(i-1)/2} \nabla_{\alpha_{(i-1)/2 - 1}}\rho e_{i-2} + \alpha_{(i+1)/2} \nabla_{\alpha_{(i+1)/2}}\rho e_{i}
    \\ &\preceq * \cdot e_{i-2} + * \cdot e_{i}
\end{align*}
\subsubsection{Term (IV)}
$$(\text{IV}) := e_1c\nabla_{c} \rho \delta_{i=1}$$
For even $i$, $\delta_{i=1} = 0$, and $$e_1c\nabla_{c} \rho \delta_{i=1} = \mathbf{0}$$
For odd $i$, 
$$e_1c\nabla_{c} \rho \delta_{i=1} \preceq * \cdot e_1$$
\subsubsection{Combining Terms (I) - (IV)}
Bringing it all together back into \eqref{eq:get_Gamma_i}, for even $i$:
\begin{align*} 
\Gamma_{ii}e_i + \Gamma^\top e_i &= -I_{\geq}e_i \circ \Big[\text{(I)} + \text{(II)}\Big] + \text{(III)}
\\
\iff \Gamma^\top e_i &= -I_{\geq}e_i \circ \Big[\text{(I)} + \text{(II)}\Big] + \text{(III)} - \Gamma_{ii}e_i 
\\
&\preceq I_{\geq}e_i \circ \Big[\begin{bmatrix} 0 \!& * \!& 0 \!& * \!& \cdots \!& 0 \!& *\end{bmatrix} ^\top + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \!& * \!& 0 \!& *  \!& \cdots \!& 0 \!& * \end{bmatrix} ^\top \Big]
    + * \cdot e_{i-2} + *\cdot e_{i}
    \\
    &= I_{\geq}e_i \circ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & * & 0 & * & \cdots & 0 & *\end{bmatrix} ^\top
\end{align*}
As can be seen, even columns of $\Gamma^\top$ are exactly zero in the odd entries. For odd $i$:
\begin{align*} 
\Gamma_{ii}e_i + \Gamma^\top e_i &= -I_{\geq}e_i \circ \Big[\text{(I)} + \text{(II)}\Big] + \text{(III)} - \text{(IV)}
\\
\iff \Gamma^\top e_i &= -I_{\geq}e_i \circ \Big[\text{(I)} + \text{(II)}\Big] + \text{(III)} - \text{(IV)} - \Gamma_{ii}e_i 
\\
&\preceq I_{\geq}e_i \circ \Big[\begin{bmatrix} * \!& 0 \!& * \!& 0 \!& \cdots \!& * \!& 0\end{bmatrix} ^\top + \begin{bmatrix} * \!& 0 \!& * \!& 0 \!& \cdots \!& * \!& 0\end{bmatrix} ^\top \Big]
    + * \cdot e_{i-2} + *\cdot e_{i} - *\cdot e_{i}
    \\
    &= I_{\geq}e_i \circ \begin{bmatrix} * & 0 & * & 0 & \cdots & * & 0\end{bmatrix} 
\end{align*}
In this case, the odd columns of $\Gamma^\top$ are exactly zero for the even entries. This proves $P_\Lambda(i) \implies P_\Gamma(i)$.


\subsection{Showing $\bigwedge_{j=i}^{2K} \big(P_\Gamma(j) \big) \!\!\implies P_\Lambda(i-1)$}
For this, we consider \eqref{eq:get_lambda_i} and plug in the sparse output of $\overline{\texttt{Bidiagonalize}}_{2K}$. We again underline the main terms with roman numerals (I) - (V), which we will investigate one by one, for even and odd $i$.
\begin{align}
    \!\!\!\!\!\begin{array}{cc}
        - \lambda_0 & \!\!i = 1 \\
      h_{i, i\!-\!1} \lambda_{i\!-\!1} & \!\!i > 1
   \end{array}\!\!\bigg\} &\!= \!\underbrace{\nabla_{\dddot{q_i}} \rho}_{(\text{I})} + \underbrace{\dddot{A}^\top \lambda_i}_{(\text{II})} - \underbrace{\!\sum_{j=i}^{K} h_{ij} \lambda_j}_{(\text{III})} + \underbrace{\dddot{Q}(\Gamma + \Gamma^\top)e_i}_{(\text{IV})} + \underbrace{\dddot{r} \gamma^\top e_i}_{(\text{V})} \label{eq:sparse_get_lambda_i}
\end{align}
We are interested in the sparsity pattern of the right-hand side, as this directly tells us about the sparsity on the left-hand side. Note that the $\lambda$s on the LHS are indexed with $i-1$ and not $i$, which is a possible point of confusion.
\subsubsection{Term (I)}
By the sparsity pattern of $\nabla_\dddot{Q} \rho$ in \eqref{eq:nabla_Q_sparsity_pattern}, we directly have 
\begin{align}
    \text{for even $i$: } & & \nabla_{\dddot{q_i}} &\preceq \begin{bmatrix} * & * & \cdots &  * & 0 & 0 &\cdots & 0\end{bmatrix} ^\top 
    \\
    \text{for odd $i$: } & & \nabla_{\dddot{q_i}} &\preceq \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & * & * & \cdots & *\end{bmatrix} ^\top
\end{align}
\subsubsection{Term (II)}
For even $i$, using the induction hypothesis:
\begin{align}
    \dddot{A}^\top \lambda_i = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} & A \\ A^\top & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} \lambda_i &\preceq \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} & A \\ A^\top & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & * & * & \cdots & *\end{bmatrix} ^\top 
    \\
    &= \begin{bmatrix} * & * & \cdots &  * & 0 & 0 &\cdots & 0\end{bmatrix} ^\top
\end{align}
For odd $i$, using the induction hypothesis:
\begin{align}
    \dddot{A}^\top \lambda_i = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} & A \\ A^\top & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} \lambda_i &\preceq \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} & A \\ A^\top & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} * & * & \cdots &  * & 0 & 0 &\cdots & 0\end{bmatrix} ^\top
    \\
    &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & * & * & \cdots & *\end{bmatrix} ^\top 
\end{align} 
\subsubsection{Term (III)}
From the sparsity of $\dddot{H}$ shown in \eqref{eq:H_sparsity_pattern}, we can simplify this sum severely (all but one $h_{ij} = 0$), and use the assumption that the induction hypothesis holds also the earlier odd $i+1$. For even $i$: 
\begin{align}
    \sum_{j=i}^{K} h_{ij} \lambda_j =  h_{i,i+1} \lambda_{i+1} \preceq  \begin{bmatrix} * & * & \cdots &  * & 0 & 0 &\cdots & 0\end{bmatrix} ^\top
\end{align}
For odd $i$: 
\begin{align}
    \sum_{j=i}^{K} h_{ij} \lambda_j =  h_{i,i+1} \lambda_{i+1} \preceq  \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots &  0 & * & * &\cdots & *\end{bmatrix} ^\top
\end{align}
\subsubsection{Term (IV)}
Here we will use the sparsity of $\dddot{Q}$ shown in \eqref{eq:Q_sparsity_pattern}, and use our induction hypothesis. The assumption $\bigwedge_{j=i}^{2K} \big(P_\Gamma(j) \big)$ gives sparsity information about the $i$th column of $\Gamma + \Gamma^\top$ due to its symmetry and triangular structure of $\Gamma$. See example \ref{ex:construction_Gamma_Gamma_top} for more. For even $i$:
\begin{align}
    \dddot{Q}(\Gamma + \Gamma^\top)e_i &= \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} & l_1 & \mathbf{0} & l_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{0} & l_k \\ 
        r_1 & \mathbf{0} & r_2 & \mathbf{0} & \cdots & r_k & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} (\Gamma + \Gamma^\top)e_i
        \\
        &\preceq \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{*} & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{*} & \cdots & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{*} \\ 
            \mathbf{*} & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{*} & \mathbf{0} & \cdots & \mathbf{*} & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & * & 0 &  * & \cdots & 0 & *\end{bmatrix} ^\top
            \\
            &= \begin{bmatrix} * & * & \cdots &  * & 0 & 0 &\cdots & 0\end{bmatrix} ^\top
\end{align}
And, for odd $i$:
\begin{align}
\dddot{Q}(\Gamma + \Gamma^\top)e_i &= \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} & l_1 & \mathbf{0} & l_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{0} & l_k \\ 
        r_1 & \mathbf{0} & r_2 & \mathbf{0} & \cdots & r_k & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} (\Gamma + \Gamma^\top)e_i
        \\
        &\preceq \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{*} & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{*} & \cdots & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{*} \\ 
            \mathbf{*} & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{*} & \mathbf{0} & \cdots & \mathbf{*} & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} * & 0 & * & 0 &  \cdots & * & 0\end{bmatrix} ^\top
            \\
            &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots &  0 & * & * &\cdots & *\end{bmatrix} ^\top
\end{align}
\subsubsection{Term (V)}
Recall the sparsity of $\gamma$ shown in \eqref{eq:gamma_sparsity_pattern} shows that $\gamma^\top e_i = 0$ for all even $i$, implying
$$\dddot{r}\gamma^\top e_i = \mathbf{0}$$
In the case of odd $i$, $\gamma^\top e_i \in \mathbb{R}$ is a scalar and, using the sparsity of $\dddot{r}$ in \eqref{eq:r_sparsity_pattern}, we get 
$$\dddot{r}\gamma^\top e_i \preceq \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots &  0 & * & * &\cdots & *\end{bmatrix} ^\top$$
\subsubsection{Combining Terms (I) - (V)}
Finally, the terms (I) - (V) of \eqref{eq:sparse_get_lambda_i} at step $i$ are recombined to obtain (a scaled) $\lambda_{i-1}$. For even $i$:
\begin{align*}
    \!\!\!\!\!\begin{array}{cc}
        - \lambda_0 & \!\!i = 1 \\
      h_{i, i\!-\!1} \lambda_{i\!-\!1} & \!\!i > 1
   \end{array}\!\!\bigg\} &= \text{(I)} + \text{(II)} - \text{(III)} + \text{(IV)} + \text{(V)}
    \\
    &\preceq (1 + 1 - 1 + 1 + 0) \begin{bmatrix} * & * & \cdots &  * & 0 & 0 &\cdots & 0\end{bmatrix} ^\top
    \\
    &\preceq \begin{bmatrix} * & * & \cdots &  * & 0 & 0 &\cdots & 0\end{bmatrix} ^\top
\end{align*}
And for odd $i$:
\begin{align*}
    \!\!\!\!\!\begin{array}{cc}
        - \lambda_0 & \!\!i = 1 \\
      h_{i, i\!-\!1} \lambda_{i\!-\!1} & \!\!i > 1
   \end{array}\!\!\bigg\} &= \text{(I)} + \text{(II)} - \text{(III)} + \text{(IV)} + \text{(V)}
    \\
    &\preceq (1 + 1 - 1 + 1 + 1) \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots &  0 & * & * &\cdots & *\end{bmatrix} ^\top
    \\
    &\preceq \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots &  0 & * & * &\cdots & *\end{bmatrix} ^\top
\end{align*}
Recall that $h_{i, i-1}$ is nonzero (see section \ref{sec:bidiag_through_hessenberg}). Dividing by $h_{i,i-1}$ preserves the sparsity pattern, so $P_\Lambda(i-1)$ holds. Together with the base case $P_\Lambda(2K)$ established in section \ref{subsec:sparsity_gamma_lambda2K}, the backward induction is complete, and the theorem is proven. \qed

\section{Alternative derivations}\label{app:alt}
For completeness, we record the classic forward constraints of Bidiagonalization and the associated adjoint system written out column by column. These are equivalent to the stable system of section~\ref{subsec:stable_adjoint_system} but are stated directly in the original coordinates rather than derived through the augmented Hessenberg detour.

\subsection{Columnwise expanded unstable adjoint}\label{app:columnwise}
Define $b_n\lambda_{n+1} := \mathbf{0}$ and $b_0\rho_0:=\mathbf{0}$
\begin{align}
0 &= \nabla l_n + a_n\lambda_n + b_n\lambda_{n+1} - A\rho_n + l_n\sigma_n
\\
0 &= \nabla r_n - A^\top\lambda_n + b_{n-1}\rho_{n-1} + a_n\rho_n + r_n\omega_n + \delta_{i,1} \kappa
\\
\rho_k &= - \nabla res
\\
\nabla a_n + l_n^\top\lambda_n + r_n^\top\rho_n &= 0 \text{ for } i \in \{1 \cdots k\}
\\
\nabla b_n + l_n^\top \lambda_{n+1} + r_{n+1}^\top \rho_{n} &= 0 \text{ for } i \in \{1 \cdots k-1\}
\\
\nabla c - \tilde r^\top\kappa &= 0
\\
\nabla \tilde r &= -c\kappa 
\\
\nabla A &= \sum_{n=1}^k -\lambda_n r_n^\top -l_n\rho_n^\top
\end{align}

\subsection{Unstable primal constraints}
Define $l_{0}b_{0} := 0$ and $res := r_{k+1} b_k $
\begin{align*}
    r_1 &= c\tilde r
    \\
    \alpha_n l_n &= Ar_n - \beta_{n-1}l_{n-1}
    \\
    \beta_n r_{n+1} &= A^\top l_n - \alpha_n r_n 
    \\
    \langle l_i, l_i \rangle &= 1 \text{ for } i \in {1 \dots k}
    \\
    \langle r_i, r_i \rangle &= 1 \text{ for } i \in {1 \dots k}
\end{align*}
Symbolically, one can show by induction that these constraints, if satisfied, imply $\langle l_i, l_j \rangle =\delta_{i,j}$ and $\langle r_i, r_j \rangle =\delta_{i,j}$. In practice however, we need to reorthonormalize the produced vectors during iteration. These classic forward constraints give rise to the adjoint system below.

\subsection{Unstable adjoint system}
This system can be derived from the bidiagonalization forward constraints directly; the columnwise form appears in section~\ref{app:columnwise}.
\begin{align}
\rho_k &= - \nabla res
\\
c \nabla c &= r_1^\top\kappa
\\
\nabla \tilde r &= -c\kappa 
\\
0 &= \nabla L - AP + \Lambda B^\top + L\Sigma
\\
0 &= \nabla R - A^\top\Lambda + PB + R\Omega + \delta_{i,1} \kappa
\\
\langle l_n, \lambda_n \rangle + \langle r_n, \rho_n\rangle &= \nabla a_n \text{ for } i \in \{1 \cdots k\}
\\
\langle l_n, \lambda_{n+1} \rangle + \langle r_{n+1}, \rho_{n}\rangle &= \nabla b_n \text{ for } i \in \{1 \cdots k-1\}
\\
\nabla A &= \sum_{n=1}^k -\lambda_n r_n^\top -l_n\rho_n^\top
\end{align}
